# Part I — Framing

## 1. Executive Summary

This paper makes two connected arguments and one uncomfortable claim.

### 1.1 On partnership

Africa is being courted simultaneously by every major power in the international system. The continent's task is not to choose a patron but to identify which relationship most reliably transfers *capability* rather than merely delivering *capacity* — the difference between learning to build and being built for.

Assessed against six axes — capability transfer, structural symmetry, openness of the technology stack, conditionality burden, geopolitical volatility, and institutional compatibility — **India emerges as the most strategically aligned bilateral partner for Africa in the artificial intelligence era.**

India is not the largest partner by volume. India–Africa trade reached approximately $93.7 billion in FY2025–26, against China–Africa trade of $348 billion in 2025 and UAE–Africa trade of $107 billion in 2024 (Annex C, rows 1–3). India is Africa's third-largest trading partner, behind the European Union and China (Diplomatist 2025).

The case for India rests instead on demonstrated capability transfer in exactly the layer that matters. The clearest single indicator: of the twenty-nine countries engaged with the Modular Open-Source Identity Platform — the open-source foundational identity system derived from India's Aadhaar architecture and maintained at IIIT-Bangalore — **approximately sixteen are in Africa, with thirteen national rollouts under way** (MOSIP 2026). Africa is not one market among many for this architecture; it is the majority of it. Togo built its national digital identity system in direct collaboration with IIIT-Bangalore. Kenya was the first African state to adopt a DigiLocker-derived document framework. Namibia became the first African country to license the Unified Payments Interface architecture, in April 2024. In February 2026, India concluded digital public infrastructure cooperation agreements covering 23 countries including six in Africa, giving access to 18 platforms through India Stack Global (Ecofin Agency 2026).

This is not a promise of technology transfer. It is a record of it, in the specific layer — identity, payments, data exchange — where architecture set now determines value capture for decades.

**The recommendation is a portfolio with explicit role assignment,** not an exclusive relationship: India as the architecture and capability partner; the Gulf as the capital and energy partner; China as the hardware and infrastructure partner; the European Union as the market and standards interface; the United States as a source of frontier research linkage and diaspora talent. What distinguishes India is that it is the only one of the five whose own strategic position — capable but capital-constrained relative to the United States and China — forces it to build openly. **Alignment that follows from structure survives changes of government; alignment that follows from goodwill does not.**

### 1.2 On method

The most consequential question in African AI policy is not which models to use or whether to train a sovereign foundation model. It is: **what can we build that allows machines to check their own work?**

Recursive self-improvement — the compounding loop in which an AI system proposes, tests, learns and proposes better — functions only where there is a cheap, fast, truthful *oracle*: a mechanism that scores a proposed solution without human judgement in the loop. Software has compilers and test suites. Mathematics has formal proof checkers. Protein engineering has structure predictors. These fields advance at extraordinary speed precisely because the feedback loop closes in seconds.

Most African development problems have no such oracle. A maize yield hypothesis takes a season to falsify. A governance reform takes a decade and cannot be run twice.

Therefore the strategy for accelerating AI-driven invention on African problems is **not to build research agents.** It is to *manufacture oracles* — benchmarks, simulators, instrumented field networks, remotely operable laboratories, data commons — and to sequence domains by how fast the loop can be made to close. Agents are the *last* layer to build, because agent scaffolding is the fastest-commoditising component in the stack. Oracles will not build themselves, and nobody outside Africa will build them.

**The evidence that this is now the right bet is real but mixed, and this paper states it as such.** On cyber capability evaluations — the domain measured most rigorously by an independent public body — the UK AI Security Institute finds open weights now four to seven months behind the closed frontier, narrowed from the six-to-ten-month gap it measured through most of 2025 (AISI 2026). On Epoch AI's broader composite index the picture is flatter: open models lag by an average of four months over January–May 2026, roughly eight points on the index, which is marginally *wider* than the three-month average Epoch measured to October 2025 (Epoch AI 2026). Epoch further cautions that its estimate may understate the gap, since open models are optimised aggressively against public benchmarks and closed laboratories do not always release their strongest systems.

**The strategic point survives the ambiguity and does not depend on resolving it.** A gap measured in months rather than years — on either measure, moving in either direction — means capability is no longer the scarce input. Chinese open-weight providers now account for over 45% of traffic through the OpenRouter aggregator, up from under 2% a year earlier (Digital Applied 2026); whatever the precise lag, the deployable frontier is now downloadable.

Capability is no longer the scarce input. The complements to capability are — data, deployment reach, domain knowledge, institutional trust — and Africa holds them.

### 1.3 The uncomfortable claim

Neither argument matters much without an intra-African precondition that has little to do with AI.

Intra-African trade stands at approximately 16% of the continent's total trade — roughly $210 billion in 2025, forecast at $230 billion in 2026 — against 59–60% for Asia and 68% for Europe (AU 2025; Afreximbank 2026; AfDB). Fifty-four fragmented markets negotiate with every partner from fifty-four weak positions, and under asymmetric bargaining every partnership defaults to extraction regardless of the goodwill across the table.

The evidence that fragmentation is already undermining continental AI ambition is direct. Analysis of the first eighteen months of Continental AI Strategy implementation found **83% of funding concentrated in four countries — Kenya, Nigeria, South Africa and Egypt** — against estimated continental needs on the order of $500 billion, with governance structures still largely aspirational (Bello 2025).

There are, however, real assets to build on, and their limitations are as instructive as their existence. The Pan-African Payment and Settlement System has been operational since 2022 and delivers savings on the order of 27% against traditional correspondent banking. **But it connects only nineteen of fifty-four countries** — the remaining thirty-five still route through correspondent banks, frequently via Europe. The rail exists; the network does not yet. That is the fragmentation problem restated inside the very system built to solve it. The AfCFTA digital trade protocol was adopted in 2025. The Africa Declaration on Artificial Intelligence was signed by 49 countries at Kigali in April 2025, committing to open datasets, sovereign compute, a continental AI market and an AI fund (Kigali Declaration 2025).

The architecture exists on paper. What this paper proposes is a specific, cheap, sequenced way to make part of it real.

## 2. The Question Behind the Question

When African policymakers ask who to partner with on AI, the question is usually heard as one about technology transfer. It is not. It is a question about **what kind of economy the continent has in 2050**, and the AI transition is the mechanism that will lock the answer in.

Consider three prior general-purpose technology transitions and what each settled.

The **industrial transition** settled the colonial economic structure: raw inputs out, manufactures in, value added abroad. The pattern outlived formal colonialism because it was embedded in physical infrastructure — rail from mines to ports rather than between cities — and in institutions.

The **containerisation transition** settled global trade routing. Ports that adapted became nodes; those that did not became spurs. Few African ports adapted in time.

The **internet transition** settled the platform economy. Africa adopted enthusiastically and captured little of the value, because the layer that captures value — identity, payments, distribution, data — was owned elsewhere. Mobile money is the instructive counter-example: value was captured domestically precisely because the rail was built and controlled domestically.

Each transition had a contestable window of roughly ten to fifteen years, after which the architecture hardened and became nearly impossible to renegotiate.

**The AI transition is in its contestable window now.** What is being settled:

- Whether foundational identity and payment infrastructure is a public good or a rented service
- Whether African data is a licensed national asset or a free input to models owned elsewhere
- Whether compute is a commodity, a concession, or a sovereign capability
- Whether models deployed across African health, agriculture, education and governance are inspectable and modifiable, or opaque and metered
- Whether African researchers are trained in Africa to solve African problems, or trained abroad to solve other people's

None of these is primarily about model quality. All are about institutional architecture, and architecture sets.

The implication for the partnership question is direct. If the decisive variable were technological sophistication, the answer is the United States. If capital, the Gulf or China. If market access, the European Union. But if the decisive variable is *which partner's involvement makes it more likely Africa ends this window owning its own architecture* — the answer changes, and it changes toward the partner whose own position forces it to build openly.

## 3. Two Failures of Imagination

### 3.1 The Leapfrog Fallacy

The mobile leapfrog was real but is badly misunderstood. It worked because mobile telephony had three properties: infrastructure cheap relative to value delivered; the unit of adoption was an individual making an individual decision; and it required almost no complementary institutional capacity. A person bought a handset and a scratch card and received full value immediately.

AI has none of these properties at the layer where value is captured. Frontier training is capital-intensive at a scale exceeding most national budgets. Deployment value depends heavily on complements — data, connectivity, electricity, institutional trust, regulatory clarity, skilled operators — which are precisely what Africa is short of. And the unit of adoption for transformative use is institutional: a health system, a land registry, a port authority.

The constraint is measurable. **Africa holds under 1% of global data centre capacity**, serving 1.4 billion people with mobile data usage growing at roughly 40% annually and a median age of 19 (360 Mozambique 2026). The continent's broader infrastructure financing gap is estimated at around $80 billion annually (Global Finance 2026).

The leapfrog framing produces a specific policy error: waiting. The continent that under-invests in complements will consume AI as a service and capture none of its surplus, exactly as it consumed the internet.

The honest framing is **compressed catch-up on the complements, combined with genuine leadership on deployment.** Africa can plausibly lead the world in *applying* AI to problems the rest of the world does not have and does not price — smallholder agronomy, informal-sector logistics, low-resource-language service delivery, off-grid energy optimisation. That is a specialisation, not a leapfrog, and it requires building.

### 3.2 The Sovereignty Fallacy

The belief that AI sovereignty means training a national foundation model is intuitively appealing and strategically close to worthless. Such a model consumes a decade of a national science budget, is obsolete within twenty-four months, requires talent concentration that does not exist domestically, and produces a system worse on almost every benchmark than freely available open weights.

Meanwhile the things that would constitute genuine sovereignty go unfunded:

- **Data sovereignty** — legal and technical control over corpora describing African populations, lands, languages, markets and biology
- **Deployment sovereignty** — ability to run, inspect, modify and audit models on infrastructure under domestic jurisdiction
- **Evaluation sovereignty** — ability to determine independently whether a system is safe and effective locally, rather than accepting a vendor's assurance
- **Talent sovereignty** — ability to train and retain people who can do all of the above
- **Standards sovereignty** — a seat where interoperability and safety norms are written

Each is achievable at a fraction of the cost of a training run, and each compounds. A national model does not compound; it depreciates.

The distinction matters for the partnership assessment, because partners differ sharply in whether they support performative sovereignty — which flatters ministers and sells hardware — or substantive sovereignty, which builds capability and reduces future dependence.

# Part II — The Partnership Assessment

## 4. Assessment Framework: Six Axes of Strategic Alignment

Partnerships are routinely assessed on volume. Volume measures the *size* of a relationship, not its *direction*. A large relationship that entrenches dependence is worse than a small one that builds capability.

**Axis 1 — Capability transfer.** Does the partnership build African capacity to do the thing, or deliver the thing? Test: after ten years, can Africans do something they previously could not, without the partner present?

**Axis 2 — Structural symmetry.** Does the partner face problems structurally similar to Africa's, such that solutions transfer? This is not sentiment but an observation about engineering: technology is shaped by the constraints under which it is designed.

**Axis 3 — Openness of the technology stack.** Are models, standards and reference implementations inspectable, modifiable, redistributable? Openness determines whether a partnership creates an asset or a subscription.

**Axis 4 — Conditionality burden.** What is required in exchange, and who bears the compliance cost?

**Axis 5 — Geopolitical volatility.** How stable is the partner's Africa policy across electoral cycles? Institutional programmes have twenty-year horizons; a partner whose commitments reverse every four years imposes a large hidden discount.

**Axis 6 — Institutional compatibility.** Does the partner's model of state–market–society relations produce institutions African states can adopt without importing pathologies they will later struggle to remove?

A seventh consideration runs across all six: **optionality.** Partnerships requiring exclusivity should be discounted heavily, because Africa's negotiating strength depends on remaining genuinely courted by all sides.

## 5. The People's Republic of China

### 5.1 What China Offers

China is, on volume, Africa's most consequential partner. Trade reached a record $348.05 billion in 2025, up 17.7% year on year (China Global South Project 2026).

**Infrastructure at scale and speed.** Chinese firms have built a very large share of Africa's terrestrial fibre, mobile network equipment, data centres, and the power and transport infrastructure digital systems depend on. Delivery on speed and cost is genuinely strong, and the alternative was frequently nothing. The scale of current flows is substantial: African countries imported 15,032 MW of Chinese solar panels in the year to 30 June 2025, a 60% increase; transformer and converter exports rose over 51%; construction machinery shipments rose 63% (NTU-SBF 2026).

**Genuinely open model weights.** This is the most under-appreciated development in African AI policy discussion. Chinese laboratories have released successive models under permissive licences whose capability approaches the closed frontier at a fraction of inference cost. Chinese open-weight providers now account for over 45% of OpenRouter traffic, up from under 2% a year earlier (Digital Applied 2026). For an African deployer this is transformative: state-of-the-art capability downloadable, runnable on domestic infrastructure, fine-tunable on local data, inspectable — no metered API, no vendor relationship, no data leaving jurisdiction. **On Axis 3, China currently scores higher than the United States.**

**Hardware supply resilience.** Development of a domestic accelerator supply chain creates a procurement path not subject to US export licensing. This is real option value.

### 5.2 The Structural Problems

**The trade pattern has not changed — it has worsened.** Africa's trade deficit with China reached $102.01 billion in 2025, having grown 64.5% in a single year. Chinese exports to Africa rose 25.8% while imports from Africa rose just 5.4% (China Global South Project 2026). Boston University's Global Development Policy Center documents that African exports to China remain dominated by extractive industries across two decades, concentrated in copper, bauxite and aluminium, chromium, manganese and cobalt (BU GDP Center 2026).

This is the *precise structure* AI-era industrial policy exists to break. A partnership reinforcing the pattern while nominally offering technology works against the strategic objective, whatever its volume.

**Financing has contracted sharply.** Chinese policy bank lending to Africa fell from a peak of $28.8 billion in 2016 to $2.1 billion in 2024 (BU GDP Center, cited in Global Finance 2026). The assumption that China is Africa's inexhaustible infrastructure financier is a decade out of date, and strategies premised on it are mis-specified.

**Capability transfer is weak.** The dominant model is engineering-procurement-construction with imported labour at skilled tiers and limited technology licensing. Assets are delivered; capability is not. On Axis 1 this is decisive.

**The institutional export problem.** Chinese digital governance embeds particular assumptions about state, platform and citizen — extensive data centralisation, integrated surveillance capability, platform-state coordination. Exported as reference architecture, these become embedded in systems African states operate for decades. The point is not deliberate imposition; it is that architecture carries politics, and this architecture is difficult to reverse once deployed across identity, payment and communications simultaneously.

**Optionality cost.** Deep integration into Chinese standards and platform stacks increasingly carries cost in relations with the US and EU, particularly in telecommunications and data. This is unfair to African states who did not create the rivalry, but it is real.

### 5.3 Assessment

| Axis | Score | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Capability transfer | Low | Turnkey delivery; limited licensing |
| Structural symmetry | Moderate | Shared development memory; now a high-capability economy |
| Stack openness | **High** | Open weights are a genuine and underrated strength |
| Conditionality burden | Low | Minimal explicit conditionality |
| Geopolitical volatility | Low | Consistent long-horizon policy |
| Institutional compatibility | **Low** | Architecture embeds governance assumptions hard to unwind |

**Verdict:** Indispensable as a hardware and infrastructure partner; increasingly valuable as a source of open model weights; poor as the primary *architecture* partner. The correct posture is to **buy the hardware, download the weights, and design the architecture elsewhere.** This is entirely feasible: open weights come with no architectural obligation.

## 6. The European Union

### 6.1 What the EU Offers

**Proximity and market size.** The EU remains Africa's largest trading partner in aggregate and its largest source of development finance and remittance flows, with shared time zones, low-latency connectivity, dense migration links, and a genuine domestic-policy interest in African prosperity.

**Regulatory sophistication and rule-of-law alignment.** European data protection and AI governance frameworks are the most developed globally, and many African states have adopted data protection regimes closely modelled on them — creating genuine interoperability. The AU's own Continental AI Strategy explicitly examines the EU AI Act among comparative regional frameworks in considering harmonised continental governance (Future of Privacy Forum 2024).

**Research infrastructure and mobility.** European research funding and university partnerships have produced substantial African research capacity.

**Genuine energy complementarity.** European decarbonisation targets and African renewable potential create real mutual interest. ECA analysis estimates AfCFTA implementation alone requires $22.4 billion in cumulative electricity infrastructure investment between 2025 and 2040, roughly 80% of it renewable (UNECA 2025).

### 6.2 The Structural Problems

**Africa is a rule-taker, not a rule-maker,** and this is sharpening. European regulatory instruments — on deforestation, carbon adjustment, supply chain due diligence, increasingly on AI — are designed through processes in which African producers have no vote, then enforced through market access. Compliance cost is borne overwhelmingly by African producers; benefit accrues to a global commons; accompanying finance is a fraction of cost imposed.

The deforestation regulation is instructive. Its objectives are legitimate. Its design assumes formality of land tenure, density of geospatial data, and administrative capacity that do not exist in the smallholder systems producing most African cocoa, rubber, coffee and palm oil. A policy aimed at large-scale commercial deforestation therefore imposes its heaviest relative burden on smallholders who were never the problem. Whatever its merits, this is not a partnership of equals.

**Europe is not a frontier AI producer.** This is decisive. With few exceptions the leading laboratories are American and Chinese; Europe's principal contribution to global AI is regulatory. A partner offering conditions but not capability cannot be the primary capability partner, by definition.

**The development-finance frame persists.** Despite repeated repositioning toward "partnership," the institutional machinery, personnel and accountability structures remain those of development assistance, shaping counterparties, horizons, metrics and implicit hierarchy.

**Fragmentation and slowness.** Internal decision processes are slow, member states pursue divergent bilateral policies, and summit commitments materialise unevenly and years late.

### 6.3 Assessment

| Axis | Score | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Capability transfer | Moderate | Strong in research; weak in industrial technology |
| Structural symmetry | **Low** | Radically different constraint environment |
| Stack openness | Moderate | Strong open-standards tradition; limited model capability |
| Conditionality burden | **High** | Extraterritorial regulation with thin accompanying finance |
| Geopolitical volatility | Moderate | Institutionally stable; politically drifting on migration |
| Institutional compatibility | High | Legal traditions genuinely transferable |

**Verdict:** Essential as a market and standards interface; not viable as primary AI capability partner. Engage the EU **as a market to be served and a standards process to be influenced** — and invest seriously in the capacity to influence it, since the alternative to shaping European regulation is absorbing it. Section 28 specifies how that capacity is built.

## 7. The United States

### 7.1 What the US Offers

**The frontier.** The most capable systems are built by American laboratories, and American universities remain the densest concentration of AI research talent.

**Capital depth.** American venture and private equity remain the deepest risk-capital pools, and Africa's most significant technology funding rounds have overwhelmingly involved American investors.

**Diaspora density and academic linkage.** The African diaspora in the United States is large, highly educated, and concentrated in exactly the technical fields that matter — a substantially underused strategic asset.

### 7.2 The Structural Problems

**The models are closed.** Leading American laboratories release capability as metered services, not artefacts. For an African deployer this means permanent vendor relationship, foreign-jurisdiction processing, unilateral pricing, no inspection or audit, no modification for local context. Every deployment builds dependence rather than assets. On the axis this paper treats as most discriminating, the United States scores worst of the five.

**Policy volatility.** American Africa policy has swung sharply across recent administrations in trade, development finance, migration, health funding and climate. The reversal of long-standing health assistance commitments demonstrated that even multi-decade programmes with strong bipartisan histories can be terminated with little notice. Any twenty-year institutional programme must apply a heavy discount. This is a planning parameter, not a moral judgement.

**Talent extraction is the dominant flow.** The principal channel through which American AI capability touches Africa is recruitment of African technical talent to American institutions — rational for individuals, negative for continental capability accumulation, with no equivalent reverse flow.

**Africa is not strategically central.** American attention is directed at China; Africa figures mainly as a theatre and a source of critical minerals. Engagement follows from rivalry rather than relationship, making it instrumental and unreliable.

**Export controls create supply risk.** American controls on advanced semiconductor exports are the single largest external constraint on African compute ambitions, and African states have no voice in their design.

### 7.3 Assessment

| Axis | Score | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Capability transfer | Low | Talent flows outward; models not transferred |
| Structural symmetry | **Very low** | Solutions built for the highest-income market on earth |
| Stack openness | **Low** | Frontier capability closed and metered |
| Conditionality burden | Moderate | Variable and politically contingent |
| Geopolitical volatility | **Very high** | Multi-decade programmes reversed within months |
| Institutional compatibility | Moderate | Strong rule-of-law tradition; extractive platform model |

**Verdict:** Valuable for frontier research linkage, risk capital and diaspora mobilisation; poor for institutional architecture. Engage at the level of universities, laboratories, diaspora networks and investors — where relationships are durable — while avoiding architectural dependence on closed American systems for anything a state must operate sovereignly.

## 8. The Gulf Cooperation Council

### 8.1 What the Gulf Offers

The Gulf is the most underestimated partner in African AI strategy, and the gap between its actual significance and its share of policy attention is striking.

**Sovereign capital at scale, moving fast.** UAE–Africa trade reached $107 billion in 2024, up 28% in a single year, making the UAE one of Africa's top five commercial partners. UAE investment into Africa exceeded $110 billion between 2019 and 2024, making it the largest single-country investor in Africa by project value. Sovereign wealth funds globally deployed $66 billion into AI and digitalisation in 2025 against combined assets of $15 trillion — with Gulf funds accounting for roughly 43% of global sovereign wealth capital and at least $23 billion of identifiable AI deployment (Mubadala $12.9bn, the Kuwait Investment Authority $6bn, the Qatar Investment Authority $4bn). Gulf investors are targeting critical minerals in the DRC and Zambia, agriculture in Ethiopia, renewable energy in Kenya and South Africa, and logistics in Egypt and Nigeria; the Africa–Middle East Corridor initiative launched in 2026 to mobilise infrastructure capital (Global Finance 2026).

**This matters more given Chinese retrenchment.** With Chinese policy bank lending down to $2.1 billion in 2024 from $28.8 billion in 2016, Gulf capital is not an alternative to Chinese finance at the margin — it is increasingly the primary available pool for African infrastructure at scale.

**Energy at the right price.** AI infrastructure is fundamentally an energy conversion business. The Gulf has the world's cheapest energy and is deploying it into compute. Africa has extraordinary renewable resource and no capital to develop it. This is the clearest complementarity available from any partner.

**Proximity and precedent.** Kenya's Comprehensive Economic Partnership Agreement with the UAE, signed January 2025, was the first such agreement between the UAE and a mainland African country — a template now available to others (Global Finance 2026).

**Serious open-model contribution.** Gulf-funded laboratories have released capable multilingual open-weight models with meaningful Arabic emphasis — directly relevant to North Africa, the Sahel and East African coastal populations, and methodologically relevant to the broader low-resource-language problem.

### 8.2 The Structural Problems

**Capital, not capability.** Gulf engagement is financial and asset-oriented. Land acquisition for food security, port and logistics stakes, compute infrastructure ownership — these build Gulf assets located in Africa. That is not African capability.

**Food security acquisition raises sovereignty questions.** Large-scale agricultural land acquisition motivated by the acquirer's food security is structurally extractive whatever the investment terms, and warrants explicit treatment in any framework agreement.

**Narrow technological base.** The Gulf is buying its way into AI capability rather than growing it; its laboratories are staffed largely by imported researchers. There is limited indigenous depth to transfer.

**Labour practice divergence** in Gulf-operated projects has attracted sustained criticism, and African labour-sending states have a direct interest complicating the relationship.

**Concentration risk.** Gulf capacity for large outbound investment remains correlated with a single volatile commodity price.

### 8.3 Assessment

| Axis | Score | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Capability transfer | Low | Financial and asset-oriented |
| Structural symmetry | Low | Radically different factor endowment |
| Stack openness | Moderate–High | Genuine open-weight contribution |
| Conditionality burden | **Very low** | Minimal governance conditionality |
| Geopolitical volatility | Low–Moderate | Stable direction; hydrocarbon-correlated capacity |
| Institutional compatibility | Low | Governance model not readily transferable |

**Verdict:** Africa's most valuable capital and energy partner for AI infrastructure; engage aggressively and specifically for that purpose. Not an architecture partner and should not be asked to be one. The optimal structure is **Gulf-financed, African-governed, jointly-architected**, with ownership and control resolved explicitly at the outset rather than discovered later.

## 9. The Republic of India

### 9.1 The Evidence

India's claim rests on being the only candidate scoring at least moderately on all six axes, and highest on the two this paper argues are decisive: structural symmetry and capability transfer. Unlike the symmetry argument, the capability-transfer argument rests on a documented record rather than on inference.

**Digital public infrastructure: the demonstrated case.**

The Modular Open-Source Identity Platform (MOSIP), developed at IIIT-Bangalore and inspired by Aadhaar, enables states to build foundational national identity on open-source, modular architecture. **Of twenty-nine country engagements, approximately sixteen are African, and thirteen national rollouts are under way** (MOSIP 2026). Implementing and pilot countries include Ethiopia, Morocco, Guinea, Niger, Sierra Leone, Burkina Faso, Madagascar, Togo, Uganda, Congo, Nigeria, the Gambia, Côte d'Ivoire, Benin, Senegal and Lesotho. An earlier Carnegie Endowment assessment (February 2025) recorded nine of eleven implementing countries as African; the absolute numbers have grown substantially since, while African participation has remained the majority of total engagement.

Critically, the mode of engagement is co-development rather than delivery. **Togo built its digital identity system in direct collaboration with IIIT-Bangalore** — the recipient ends up able to operate and modify the system (House of UPSC 2025).

On payments: **Namibia became the first African country to license UPI architecture, in April 2024**, through an agreement between its central bank and NPCI International. India committed to share UPI experience with Ghana during 2025, with Uganda, Rwanda and Mozambique expressing interest. **Kenya was the first African country to adopt a DigiLocker framework** (IMPRI 2025; Politics for India 2026).

In February 2026, India announced DPI cooperation agreements covering 23 countries including six in Africa, providing access to 18 platforms via India Stack Global — spanning identity, payments, document wallets, hospital management, judicial digitisation, learning, vaccination management, telemedicine, procurement, public finance management and infrastructure planning (Ecofin Agency 2026).

The scale behind the offer is not trivial. Over 95% of India's population holds a digital ID; UPI processes on the order of 18–20 billion transactions monthly; roughly half of all real-time digital transactions globally occur in India (ORF 2025; Global Fintech Fest 2025).

**Skills, sited in Africa.** IIT Madras opened its first overseas campus in Zanzibar in 2023, offering degrees in AI and data science (House of UPSC 2025). The e-VBAB tele-education network holds MOUs with 11 African countries. This is the reverse of the talent-extraction pattern: capacity built in-country rather than students exported.

**Development finance.** India extended over $13 billion in concessional assistance for African projects between 2010 and 2024, and has invested approximately $75 billion in Africa with a stated target of $150 billion by 2030 (IMPRI 2025; Diplomatist 2025). India is described as Africa's second-largest lender.

### 9.2 The Structural Symmetry Argument

India is building digital systems under constraints recognisably Africa's:

- Very large populations at low average income, where a service costing two dollars a month is premium
- Intermittent connectivity, device sharing, reliance on low-end handsets
- Large informal sectors leaving no formal record
- Multilingual populations where the language of government is not the language of the household, and dozens of languages have limited digital corpora
- Thin historical formal identity coverage, and the necessity of building identity at population scale
- Public health and agricultural extension chronically short of trained personnel
- Fiscal constraints making per-capita-expensive solutions politically impossible

Technology is shaped by the constraints under which it is designed. A diagnostic tool built for a hospital with a radiologist assumes a radiologist. A crop advisory built for a farmer with a smartphone, a bank account and a fixed field boundary assumes all three. Indian systems are designed under African-like constraints, which means they *port* — not perfectly, but at a fraction of the adaptation cost of systems designed in San Francisco, Brussels or Shenzhen.

The MOSIP adoption pattern is the empirical test of this claim, and it passes: African countries forming the majority of engagements with this architecture — and the majority of live national rollouts — is not what one would observe if Indian architecture were merely one option among several equally transferable ones.

### 9.3 The Open-Source Structural Argument

India cannot outspend the United States or China on frontier training and knows it. Its AI strategy consequently prioritises shared national compute accessible to startups and researchers, open datasets, open model releases and language-technology investment — because openness is the only strategy available to a capable but capital-constrained actor.

This matters because **India's openness is not a policy preference that could reverse with a change of government. It is the strategy dictated by India's position.** Africa occupies the same position. That makes India a co-strategist rather than a vendor — alignment by structure, not goodwill, which is the only kind that survives contact with politics.

Section 12.4 examines the conditions under which this argument fails.

### 9.4 Shared Technical Agenda

**Low-resource languages.** India's central technical AI challenge — capable systems for languages with limited digital text, spoken by large populations, in voice-first contexts — is *identically* Africa's. Corpus-building methodology, transfer learning, speech-first interface design, and evaluation frameworks for languages without standard benchmarks are directly shared work. This is one of very few areas where the research agenda is genuinely joint rather than donor-recipient.

**Frugal engineering.** India has institutionalised achieving acceptable performance at radically lower cost — in space technology, generic pharmaceuticals, medical devices, payment infrastructure. This is a transferable engineering *culture*, and it is the culture African deployment requires.

**Pharmaceutical precedent.** India's generic pharmaceutical industry is the working proof that a developing economy can build globally competitive capability in a technologically demanding sector, and its role supplying affordable medicines to Africa is arguably the most consequential positive health intervention of the past three decades. The template — local manufacture, technology licensing, price competition, regulatory capacity building — is directly applicable and already partially replicated in African pharmaceutical manufacturing. Indian pharmaceutical exports run at over $12 billion in a five-month period, with South Africa a significant destination (IBEF 2026).

**Complementary demography.** Both are young. Both face the question of whether AI destroys the labour-intensive pathways that historically absorbed young workforces. This is a shared strategic problem — arguably the most important economic question either faces — creating a basis for joint policy research rather than technical assistance. Section 29 treats it directly.

**No colonial ledger; a genuine diaspora bridge.** India engages without the historical baggage complicating European engagement and without the great-power framing making American and Chinese engagement instrumental. Long-established Indian-origin communities across East and Southern Africa provide dense functional commercial and social bridges.

### 9.5 The Honest Weaknesses

**India cannot match Chinese or Gulf capital.** $13 billion in concessional assistance over fourteen years is a fraction of Gulf deployment or historical Chinese lending. India offers architecture, method and capability — not the balance sheet. Any strategy relying on India must source capital elsewhere, which is precisely why this paper recommends a portfolio.

**India's own compute is modest.** India is not a frontier compute power, and cannot solve Africa's compute access problem because it has not fully solved its own.

**The trade relationship remains commodity-weighted and is currently in deficit for Africa's partner, not Africa.** India–Africa trade in FY2025–26 was $93.69 billion, with Indian exports of $45.42 billion against imports of $48.27 billion — India runs a deficit, driven substantially by hydrocarbons and raw commodity inflows (IANS 2026). India's own Commerce Minister has publicly stated the need to move beyond raw material trade toward higher-value products, integrated supply chains and local manufacturing. The structural composition is not yet transformed, and intellectual honesty requires saying so. The difference from the China case is directional rather than absolute: African exports to India are commodity-weighted, but the deficit runs the other way and the technology relationship is genuinely more collaborative.

**Domestic priorities dominate.** India has several hundred million citizens in poverty. When resources are constrained, Africa will not be prioritised.

**Implementation quality varies.** Indian project delivery in Africa, particularly under credit line programmes, has a mixed record on timeliness and quality relative to Chinese execution.

**DPI is not politically neutral.** Population-scale digital identity carries genuine risks: exclusion of those failing enrolment or authentication, surveillance potential, function creep. India's own experience includes documented benefit denial arising from authentication failure. The World Economic Forum has flagged cybersecurity and systemic risk in large-scale DPI deployment (Ecofin Agency 2026). Any African adoption must build in exclusion safeguards, offline fallbacks, purpose limitation and independent oversight from the outset — treating India's difficulties as valuable evidence rather than as an argument against the model. **Adopt the architecture; improve the safeguards.** MOSIP's open-source character is what makes improving the safeguards possible; a closed system would not permit it.

**Not all Indian engagement is benign.** Indian commercial actors in Africa pursue commercial interests, including in extractive sectors and in land, with the same behavioural range as any other commercial actor. State-level alignment does not extend automatically to every private relationship.

## 11. The Portfolio Doctrine

| Partner | Assigned role | What to avoid |
|---|---|---|
| **India** | Architecture, DPI, capability transfer, joint research, language technology, skills siting | Over-reliance for capital or compute |
| **Gulf** | Capital, energy, compute financing, regional data centre anchoring | Ceding ownership and control; land acquisition without safeguards |

Three governing rules:

**Rule 1 — Never grant exclusivity in the architecture layer.** Hardware and finance may be sole-sourced on price. Identity, payments, data exchange and model deployment must remain multi-vendor and open-standard, or bargaining power is permanently lost.

**Rule 2 — Negotiate as a bloc wherever the subject is standards.** Individual states have no leverage over European regulation, American export controls or platform terms. Continental bodies have some. The differential is the largest available return on institutional investment.

**Rule 3 — Assign roles explicitly and publicly.** Ambiguity about what each partner is for produces mission creep, and mission creep in the architecture layer is how dependence is acquired accidentally.

## 12. Falsification Conditions: Where This Analysis Could Be Wrong

A recommendation that cannot be falsified is not analysis. Four conditions would change the conclusion, in descending order of probability.

### 12.1 If the open-closed capability gap reopens decisively

This is the assumption on which the entire leverage model rests, and it is the one most worth monitoring.

Current evidence is genuinely mixed, and the two most rigorous measures point in different directions. The UK AI Security Institute measures a four-to-seven-month gap on cyber tasks, narrower than the six-to-ten months measured through most of 2025 (AISI 2026) — that is narrowing, and it is the more granular measure. Epoch AI measures the best open-weight models lagging the closed frontier by an average of four months over January–May 2026, roughly eight points on its composite index with a 90% confidence interval of seven to eleven points (Epoch AI 2026) — but that is marginally *wider* than the three-month average Epoch measured between January 2023 and October 2025. On the composite measure the gap has slightly widened.

The evidence is therefore not unambiguous, and this paper does not overstate it. Epoch itself warns that its estimate may understate the true gap on two counts: public test sets are easier to optimise against, so public benchmarks may flatter open models (WhatLLM 2026); and leading closed laboratories do not always release their most capable systems. Closed models retain a measurable lead on reasoning-heavy evaluation, typically three to eight percentage points on frontier reasoning and mathematics benchmarks (Digital Applied 2026). The remaining closed-model advantage is specific and concentrated in **long-horizon agentic reasoning** — tasks running twenty to thirty dependent steps where an early error must be caught and corrected rather than compounded (Perform Digital 2026).

That last point deserves emphasis, because it cuts directly against Part IV of this paper. **Long-horizon agentic reasoning is precisely the capability an autonomous research agent requires.** The domain where open weights remain weakest is the domain where the recursive research architecture needs them strongest. AISI further notes that April 2026 saw some of the largest single-generation gains in closed-model capability since testing began, and that it is not yet clear whether open weights will match them (The Decoder 2026).

**The honest position:** open weights are unambiguously sufficient for the *deployment* agenda in Part III — classification, extraction, diagnosis, translation, forecasting, advisory. They are currently marginal for the fully autonomous *research* agenda in Part IV. This is an argument for building the oracle layer now, since oracles are agent-agnostic and will be waiting when agent capability arrives — not an argument for delay. But a paper claiming otherwise would be overselling.

**Falsification test:** if the Epoch gap widens beyond twelve months for two consecutive years, or if major open-weight releases cease, the leverage model requires fundamental revision and the assessment shifts materially toward the United States. The one-month widening recorded between the October 2025 and May 2026 measurements does not approach this threshold, but it is the first movement in the wrong direction since the series began and should be tracked at each Epoch release rather than assumed away.

### 12.2 If speed and hard infrastructure dominate the objective function

China is then the correct answer and it is not close. A government judging it has five years, not twenty, to deliver visible infrastructure should choose China, accept the architectural cost, and plan to renegotiate later from a position of having something rather than nothing. This is a defensible weighting, not an error.

The counter-consideration is that Chinese lending has contracted from $28.8 billion in 2016 to $2.1 billion in 2024 — the speed advantage is real but the financing volume behind it is a fraction of what it was.

### 12.3 If short-run export earnings dominate

The EU remains the correct primary partner, because it is the market that pays now for what Africa currently sells. A strategy optimising for institutional architecture at the cost of near-term export revenue is politically unsustainable in most African fiscal contexts.

### 12.4 If India's openness weakens

The structural argument in 9.3 holds only while India remains capital-constrained relative to the United States and China. Should Indian laboratories reach genuine frontier capability, commercial incentives could shift toward closure, and the alignment this paper treats as structural would become merely contingent.

**Falsification test:** if NPCI International shifts UPI licensing toward proprietary terms, or if MOSIP governance moves away from open-source maintenance, the capability-transfer case weakens substantially. Note that one source characterises UPI's export as proceeding under bilateral conditions rather than as a pure public good (House of UPSC 2025); the open-source status of MOSIP is unambiguous, that of the payments layer somewhat less so. This distinction should be tested explicitly in any framework negotiation.

# Part III — The Leverage Model

## 13. Open Source as Structural Advantage

### 13.1 The Core Economic Argument

AI's competitive structure decomposes into two layers with radically different economics.

The **capability layer** — foundation model training — is extremely capital-intensive, extremely talent-concentrated, and has near-zero marginal reproduction cost once a model exists. It exhibits winner-take-most dynamics and Africa cannot compete in it. That is arithmetic, not defeatism.

The **deployment layer** — turning capability into value in a specific context — is low capital intensity and highly dependent on inherently local assets: proprietary data, distribution reach, domain knowledge, regulatory relationships, institutional trust, last-mile human infrastructure.

**Open weights collapse the capability layer's cost to approximately zero while leaving the deployment layer entirely intact.** The market evidence is now unambiguous: Chinese open-weight providers alone account for over 45% of OpenRouter traffic, up from under 2% a year earlier, and leading open models cost as much as fifty times less per million output tokens than closed frontier equivalents on comparable coding workloads (Digital Applied 2026).

This is the most consequential structural development for African AI strategy in five years, and it is systematically underweighted because policy attention tracks the capability layer, where the headlines are.

The implication is precise: **the scarce input is no longer intelligence; it is the complements to intelligence.** Africa holds complements no foreign laboratory can acquire — how smallholder agronomy actually works in a given agro-ecological zone, the languages people actually speak, the trust relationships through which advice is actually accepted, the informal networks through which goods actually move.

### 13.2 The Doctrine

**Adopt open weights; never license closed capability for sovereign functions.** Any system a state must operate — identity, benefits, health records, land registry, taxation, elections — must run on models that can be inspected, audited, modified and operated on domestic infrastructure. This is a sovereignty requirement, not a cost preference. Commercial and non-critical applications may use whatever performs best.

**Compete on fine-tuning, data and deployment, not pre-training.** Value-added activity is: building high-quality local-language and domain corpora; fine-tuning open base models; building evaluation suites reflecting local conditions; constructing last-mile delivery to a farmer, clinic or trader.

**Treat data as a licensed national asset** (section 15).

**Pool compute regionally** (section 16).

**Publish aggressively.** Every benchmark, corpus, evaluation and reference implementation released openly recruits global effort at zero cost — the highest-return institutional behaviour available (Part IV).

---

## 14. What Open Weights Do and Do Not Give You

**What they genuinely provide:** near-frontier capability at zero licence cost; operation entirely within domestic jurisdiction; full inspectability; fine-tuning on proprietary data without disclosure; freedom from unilateral pricing changes and deprecation; durable institutional assets rather than subscriptions.

**What they do not provide:**

*Freedom from compute costs.* Weights are free; inference is not. At scale, serving costs dominate, denominated in hardware and electricity. This is the real constraint, and why energy and compute financing feature so heavily in the partnership recommendations.

*Engineering capability.* Downloading a model and operating a reliable production system are separated by a large body of unglamorous skill — serving infrastructure, monitoring, evaluation, versioning, safety filtering, incident response. Scarce, and must be deliberately built.

*Frontier parity in every domain.* Open models lag on the hardest reasoning and long-horizon agentic tasks by roughly three to eight percentage points on reasoning benchmarks, with the gap concentrated in multi-step coherence (Digital Applied 2026; Perform Digital 2026). Not decisive for most deployment use cases; decisive for the autonomous research agenda, as section 12.1 concedes.

*Safety by default.* Open weights can be fine-tuned to remove safety behaviours, and AISI has found that safety measures on open models are largely ineffective against determined modification (The Decoder 2026). A continent deploying open models at scale acquires a genuine safety obligation that closed-API consumers outsource to the vendor. This requires domestic evaluation capacity — conveniently, the same capacity needed for evaluation sovereignty and for benchmark credibility.

*Supply security.* Open weights are released at the discretion of the releasing laboratory. Should major open releases cease, existing weights remain usable but the frontier moves away. A hedge — participation in community-governed open model efforts, and retention of technical capacity to continue training from existing checkpoints — is prudent.

## 15. The Data Sovereignty Question

Data is the asset Africa actually holds, and it is currently being given away. The pattern is familiar from extractive industries: a raw input of enormous value leaves unpriced, is transformed elsewhere, and returns as an expensive finished product.

### 15.1 What a Data Regime Should Establish

**Categorisation.** *Open public data* (weather, published statistics, geological surveys) released freely to maximise use; *licensed strategic data* (agricultural, health, biodiversity, language, logistics) under terms including fee, purpose limitation and reciprocity; *protected personal data* under strict privacy regimes with consent architecture; *restricted sovereign data* (defence, critical infrastructure) not externally available.

**Reciprocity as the core instrument.** The most powerful licensing term is not fee but obligation: any model trained on licensed African data must be released under an open licence usable within Africa, and must be evaluated for performance on African populations before deployment. This converts data from one-way outflow into a mechanism for acquiring capability.

**Consent that actually functions.** Consent architecture of the kind DPI implements through consent managers allows individuals to grant and revoke specific, purpose-limited, time-limited access. Technically solved; requires adoption. Community-level consent, particularly for indigenous knowledge, is less solved and requires genuine work.

**Benefit-sharing that reaches the source.** A farmer whose field data trains a yield model, or a community whose recordings train a speech system, should receive value. Mechanisms exist — royalty pools, free service access, cooperative equity — but require deliberate design.

**Harmonisation across borders.** Fifty-four incompatible regimes make continental data assets unusable and advantage actors who can afford fifty-four compliance functions.

### 15.2 The Instrument Already Exists

This is not a proposal for new machinery. The AfCFTA Protocol on Digital Trade was adopted in 2025, establishing continental data governance standards and e-commerce frameworks addressing data privacy harmonisation, consumer protection and cybersecurity across diverse legal systems (Discovery Alert 2026). The AU has issued African Guidelines on Integrating Data Provisions in Digital Trade Protocols (AU 2026).

**The recommendation is therefore to populate an existing instrument with the reciprocity obligation, not to build a new one.** This is a materially easier political ask than it appears, because the vehicle is already ratified.

### 15.3 The Enforcement Problem

Data regimes are difficult to enforce against actors outside jurisdiction, and this should be acknowledged rather than wished away. Realistic levers: market access (a firm wishing to operate in African markets can be bound); procurement (public purchasing can require compliance); platform-level agreements (a small number of actors control most collection); and collective action (individually unenforceable terms become enforceable applied by a bloc).

The last is decisive and returns to the central theme: fragmentation makes every good policy unenforceable. AfCFTA's $3.4 trillion integrated market across 54 economies is the leverage; it is currently unused for this purpose (Global Finance 2026).

## 16. Compute: Pooling, Not Sovereignty Theatre

### 16.1 The Case Against National Clusters

Several African states have announced national AI compute facilities. Most will be poor investments, for structural reasons.

A modern accelerator cluster is economic only at high sustained utilisation, which requires a deep pipeline of demand. Almost no individual African country has a demand curve deep enough to justify a facility at efficient scale. The predictable outcome is expensive hardware at low utilisation, depreciating on roughly a three-year effective cycle, with insufficient operating budget for the specialised staff required.

The concentration data already shows this dynamic in continental AI funding: 83% concentrated in four countries during the first eighteen months of Continental AI Strategy implementation (Bello 2025). Duplicating sub-scale facilities across the remaining fifty would compound rather than correct it.

### 16.2 The Case for Pooling

Pooling addresses each problem. Aggregated demand reaches efficient scale. Siting follows energy availability rather than political geography. Operating costs are shared. Specialist staff have a career at a facility large enough to sustain one. Procurement volume improves terms materially.

**Design principles:**

*Site on energy, not politics.* Locate where generation is abundant, reliable and low-carbon — geothermal, hydro, high-quality solar with storage. Data moves cheaply; electricity does not.

*Finance externally, govern continentally, operate professionally.* Gulf capital suits this asset class: Gulf funds hold roughly 43% of global sovereign wealth capital and deployed at least $23 billion into AI and digitalisation in 2025. Governance must be African and multilateral, with ownership and control fixed at the outset. Operation should be professional and insulated from allocation politics.

*Allocate by merit through transparent process.* Peer-reviewed allocation with published decisions, a guaranteed share for early-career and small-institution researchers, and a commercial tier cross-subsidising research use.

*Build for inference first.* The dominant African need is serving models, not training them. Inference is far less demanding on interconnect and runs on cheaper, more available hardware. Fine-tuning is second. Pre-training is a distant third and may never be justified.

*Federate rather than centralise.* Three to five regional facilities with high-quality interconnection is more robust than one continental facility, and far more politically achievable.

### 16.3 The Connectivity Precondition

Pooled compute is useless without terrestrial fibre to reach it. Intra-African bandwidth remains expensive and poorly meshed, with traffic between neighbouring African countries persistently routing through Europe. Regional internet exchange points and cross-border terrestrial capacity are unglamorous prerequisites and should be funded as such.

# Part IV — The Recursive Research Architecture

## 17. The Binding Constraint Is the Oracle

### 17.1 What Recursive Improvement Requires

The loop has four components: a *proposer* generating candidate solutions; an *evaluator* scoring them; a *selector* retaining what works; a *learner* updating the proposer. The proposer is now cheap and improving rapidly, supplied by open weights. Selector and learner are engineering. **The evaluator — the oracle — is the constraint.**

An oracle scores a proposed solution *without a human in the loop*, at low cost, quickly, truthfully. Its three properties: **latency**, **cost per evaluation**, and **fidelity** to real-world performance.

Fields advancing fastest have excellent oracles. Software: compilers, type systems, test suites — seconds, near-zero cost, high fidelity. Mathematics: formal proof checkers — seconds, near-zero, perfect. Structural biology: learned structure predictors — minutes, low, good and improving.

Fields advancing slowly are those where the oracle is a human expert, a clinical trial, a growing season, or an election.

### 17.2 The Strategic Inversion

**The correct sequence is not: build agents, then apply them. It is: build oracles, then let agents — commoditising rapidly and better next year — run against them.**

*Agent capability improves exogenously.* Anything built today is superseded within eighteen months by freely available systems. Oracles do not depreciate: a rigorous benchmark for cassava disease detection or minigrid dispatch remains valuable indefinitely and *appreciates* as more systems are measured against it.

*Oracles are what nobody else will build.* Global laboratories build oracles for problems they care about. Nobody outside Africa will build a rigorous benchmark for smallholder intercropping under variable rainfall, informal cross-border trade routing, or low-cost housing thermal performance in humid tropical conditions. If Africa does not, they do not exist — and the powerful general systems arriving this decade will have nothing to optimise against in African problem space.

*A published oracle recruits global effort at zero marginal cost.* This is the highest-leverage move in this paper. Publishing a rigorous, documented, machine-scorable benchmark causes researchers worldwide — in laboratories with budgets Africa cannot approach — to optimise against African problems, at their own expense, for their own reasons of prestige and publication. The history of machine learning is the history of benchmarks pulling entire fields toward whatever was measured. Africa has never systematically used this mechanism.

There is early evidence the gap is being noticed: recent published work explicitly identifies the absence of machine learning applied to post-harvest loss prediction as a research gap in West Africa, noting that models could identify high-risk communities before losses occur (Sierra Leone rice yield study, arXiv 2026). That is a benchmark waiting to be written.

### 17.3 The Honest Limitation

Section 12.1 established that the capability open weights currently lack most is long-horizon agentic reasoning — exactly what autonomous research requires. This paper therefore does **not** claim that agents can autonomously invent in these domains today. It claims that the oracle layer is agent-agnostic, cheap, non-depreciating, and will be the binding constraint whenever agent capability does arrive.

**The wager is: build the substrate now, harvest when capability lands.** If capability never lands, the oracle layer is still independently valuable — benchmarks and data commons improve human research regardless. This is an unusually favourable risk profile for a technology bet, and it is the reason the recommendation survives even under pessimistic assumptions about agent progress.

## 19. The Fast-Loop Domains

### 19.1 Logistics and Trade Infrastructure

**Why first.** Near-pure simulation domain. Routing, scheduling, inventory placement, cold chain design, port operations, customs sequencing and modal choice are mathematically specifiable, and a calibrated simulator scores a proposal in milliseconds. An agent evaluates millions of candidate designs overnight. No other domain offers this.

**The economic prize is exceptional and measurable.** Post-harvest losses across Africa run at 30–50% of production, with sub-Saharan losses valued at over $4 billion annually on older FAO estimates and as high as $48 billion on recent cold-chain-inclusive assessments; fruit and vegetable losses reach 40–45% (WFP; FAO; BOH Infrastructure 2026; Ghana and Ethiopia value chain studies). In some countries logistical inefficiency raises staple food costs by as much as 75% (TechCabal 2025).

Africa imports over $50 billion in food annually while losing this much of its own production downstream. Practitioners estimate that even a 1% reduction in post-harvest loss could yield $40 million annually (TechCabal 2025). Logistics inefficiency is arguably the single largest tax on African economic activity, and it is a *computational* problem to a degree few other constraints are.

The demonstrated tractability is instructive. Kenya's cold chain investments — expanded reefer capacity at Mombasa, solar-powered storage in avocado regions, pre-cooling at packhouses — reduced spoilage for perishables by up to 40%, and Kenya Ports Authority reefer upgrades cut perishable dwell time from seven days to 48 hours (Maersk 2025). These gains came from infrastructure and coordination, not from novel technology. Optimisation over the coordination layer is exactly what simulation-driven search does well.

**Concrete build: a continental freight and trade digital twin.** Road, rail, port and border infrastructure with real capacity and reliability parameters; commodity flow data; cost and time functions calibrated against observed shipments; and critically a representation of *informal* cross-border trade, invisible in official statistics and enormous in reality.

Search targets against the twin: routing and consolidation; cold chain design under specified loss tolerances; siting of aggregation centres, dry ports and border facilities; customs and inspection sequencing; and corridor investment prioritisation — which single infrastructure investment most reduces continental logistics cost per dollar spent.

**Why the loop closes for real.** Unlike simulations of social processes, logistics simulations can be *validated continuously* against observed shipments. Every consignment moved recalibrates the twin, improving fidelity, improving what agents discover. The loop is recursive at the level of the oracle itself.

**Immediate step.** Instrument freight: GPS traces, dwell times, cost declarations, delay reasons. Trade associations, exporter cooperatives and logistics firms can generate this at low cost.

**Institutional fit.** This connects directly to live continental machinery. PAPSS delivers savings of roughly 27% against correspondent banking but currently connects only nineteen of fifty-four countries; non-tariff barrier elimination along key corridors is already an AfCFTA workstream (Afreximbank 2026). A freight twin is a decision-support tool for work already mandated — and corridor prioritisation is precisely the question of where the remaining thirty-five connections are worth making first.

### 19.2 Connectivity

**Why fast.** Radio propagation, network topology and traffic engineering are modellable to high fidelity. Coverage, capacity and cost for a proposed design are computable without building anything.

**Unexplored search space:** dynamic spectrum allocation and TV whitespace for rural coverage; mesh and community topologies for settlements below commercial viability; solar-and-storage base station siting under constrained backhaul; hybrid terrestrial–satellite routing; caching and edge placement reducing expensive international transit.

**The oracle:** propagation and network simulators calibrated against measurement, with crowdsourced signal data from ordinary handsets providing cheap calibration input.

**The regulatory complement.** Much value here is unlocked by regulation rather than technology — spectrum policy permitting dynamic access, infrastructure sharing mandates, licensing accommodating community networks. Agent-discovered designs that are illegal to deploy are worthless, so regulatory sandbox provision belongs in the programme from the start.

### 19.3 Energy

**Why fast.** Power systems are among the best-characterised systems in engineering, and the models are mature.

**High-value search:** minigrid sizing and dispatch under demand uncertainty, where over-sizing wastes scarce capital and under-sizing destroys reliability and willingness to pay; productive-use load design, the actual determinant of minigrid economics; demand forecasting from sparse history; degradation-aware storage dispatch; grid-edge coordination; and co-location of compute load with renewable generation.

**The oracle:** power system simulators validated against smart meter telemetry. Every metered minigrid is a live experiment improving the model.

**The strategic connection.** African renewable resource is abundant and under-financed largely because reliable demand is insufficient. AI compute is an ideal anchor load — large, continuous, location-flexible, creditworthy. With ECA estimating $22.4 billion of electricity infrastructure investment required for AfCFTA implementation to 2040, roughly 80% renewable (UNECA 2025), optimising joint siting of generation and compute is a research problem with immediate financing consequences.

### 19.4 Housing and the Built Environment

**Why fast.** Structural performance, thermal behaviour, daylighting, ventilation, material quantity and cost are computable. Finite element analysis, computational fluid dynamics and energy simulation provide direct scoring functions.

**Genuinely under-explored search space:** generative structural design over locally available materials — stabilised earth, laterite, bamboo, timber, agricultural waste binders, recycled aggregate — where global research effort has been minimal because these materials are not used in high-income construction; passive thermal design for humid tropical and hot arid conditions where imported typologies perform badly and cooling is unaffordable; incremental construction sequences matching how households actually build, in stages as funds allow; modular components manufacturable in small local workshops; settlement-scale layout optimisation for access, drainage, ventilation and infrastructure cost.

**Why this domain merits attention.** Housing is where the gap between what is designed and what people can afford is widest, and where design constraints are most local. It is simultaneously highly computable and almost entirely unaddressed by global research effort — an unusual combination.

**Two honest caveats.** First, on economic weight housing ranks below agriculture and health, and its position in the sequence rests on tractability and neglect rather than magnitude; it is placed here because it is cheap to start, not because it matters most. Second, simulation fidelity for novel material combinations is limited, because property data for stabilised earth, bamboo and agri-waste composites is thin compared to steel and concrete. This makes a *physical* testing complement mandatory — a materials characterisation facility feeding properties back into simulation. It is the cheapest laboratory in the programme and should be built first (Annex B).

### 19.5 Health Diagnostics

**Why diagnostics specifically.** Diagnostic model development has an excellent oracle: a labelled retrospective dataset scores a proposal against thousands of cases instantly. The loop structure is solved; the constraint is purely data.

**High-value targets:** point-of-care imaging where specialist scarcity binds — radiology, dermatology, ophthalmic screening, cervical screening; acoustic diagnostics from cough and breath; low-cost microscopy for malaria, tuberculosis and parasitic disease; triage for community health workers without physician backup.

**The critical requirement: locally-representative labelled data.** Models trained on high-income-country datasets underperform on African populations owing to prevalence differences, comorbidity patterns, equipment differences, skin tone representation, and population genetics. Building African clinical datasets with high-quality labelling is the highest-value health investment here — and it is an *oracle* investment, not a model investment.

**Deployment is a separate and harder problem** involving regulatory approval, clinician trust, workflow integration and liability. The research loop and deployment pathway should be resourced separately and not conflated.

## 20. The Medium-Loop Domains

### 20.1 Agriculture

Agriculture has the most at stake and the most acute latency problem. A hypothesis about variety, spacing, timing, input regime or intercropping takes a full season to test, capping sequential iterations at one or two per year.

**The solution is not to make the loop faster. It is to make it wider.**

If a single trial takes a season but ten thousand run in parallel across every agro-ecological zone, *information yield per season* becomes enormous. This is a fundamentally different architecture from the research station model, which runs few highly controlled trials in unrepresentative conditions.

**The distributed trial network.** Farmer cooperatives become the experimental array. Each participating farm runs an assigned treatment on a defined plot with standardised measurement. Requirements: randomised treatment assignment; standardised low-burden capture — a photo, a GPS point, a short voice note in the farmer's language; low-cost sensing where affordable and satellite-derived proxies where not; and honest incentive design, because farmers bear real risk in planting something other than what they would have chosen.

**Why cooperatives are the right unit.** They exist, hold trust relationships, aggregate for input purchase and output marketing, and have communication channels. A cooperative that distributes inputs can distribute treatments; one that aggregates produce can aggregate data. The marginal cost of adding an experimental function to an existing cooperative is far below building a research network from nothing.

**The equity requirement — non-negotiable.** This design places experimental risk on people with very little buffer. It requires informed consent in the farmer's language; insurance or guaranteed compensation against below-baseline yield on trial plots; priority access to results; and data rights keeping value with the cooperative rather than transferring it to whoever funds the programme.

**The complementary sensing layer.** Satellite vegetation indices, soil moisture and weather reanalysis give continuous low-cost observation across the network. Drone phenotyping gives higher resolution for a subset. Low-cost soil sensors close the input side. Together these convert a slow annual oracle into a continuous one producing informative signal throughout the season.

**Where the loop is already fast within agriculture:** crop and pest disease identification from images; yield prediction from remote sensing scored against harvest records; market price forecasting scored daily; irrigation scheduling scored against soil moisture within days; post-harvest loss prediction scored within weeks. Prosecute these immediately using fast-loop methods while the trial network is built for genuinely seasonal questions.

**Genetics and breeding.** Genomic selection is an important medium-term opportunity — genotype-to-phenotype models score against existing breeding programme records, and the loop is fast in silico even though field validation remains seasonal. African genomic resources for cassava, sorghum, millet, cowpea, teff, yam and plantain are thin relative to maize, wheat and rice. Building them is a defined, tractable, high-value oracle investment.

### 20.2 Environmental Sustainability

**Fast where observation is remote.** Deforestation detection, land cover change, water body monitoring, air quality estimation, coastal erosion and burn scar mapping have satellite oracles with day-scale latency.

**Slow where attribution matters.** Whether a specific intervention caused a specific ecological outcome requires long observation and careful counterfactual construction. This part is not recursive.

**The high-value application is compliance and evidence generation.** Environmental monitoring capability converts directly into market access — traceability, deforestation-free verification, carbon measurement — and therefore has a revenue model most research lacks. The same geospatial capability that answers a research question answers a European regulator. Building it once serves both, which is the practical bridge between section 6's diagnosis and section 28's remedy.

### 20.3 Education

Education is one of the three priority sectors the Continental AI Strategy names alongside agriculture and health, and a paper that triages by loop latency owes an account of why it does not appear among the fast-loop domains rather than simply omitting it.

**The loop is genuinely slow, and the reason is instructive.** The outcome that matters — durable learning, and beyond that the capacity to reason and create — is measurable only over years, is confounded by everything else in a child's life, and cannot be run twice on the same learner. Worse, the proxies that *are* fast to measure are the ones most easily gamed: a system optimised against immediate test performance will produce immediate test performance, which is not the objective. The Strategy is explicit about this hazard, warning that AI *must not threaten teachers' rights and undermine learners' thinking processes and creativity, which in turn negatively affect innovation.* This is precisely a warning about optimising a bad oracle, and it should be read as one.

**Three sub-problems do have usable oracles**, and these are where the method applies:

*Language and access.* Speech recognition, translation and voice-first interfaces for instruction in the learner's home language score instantly against labelled data. This is the same corpus investment described in Layer 2, and education is one of its highest-value consumers. The Strategy names *language diversity* as a specific challenge for African education; it is a data problem before it is a pedagogy problem.

*Accessibility tooling.* Dyslexia screening, text-to-sign-language translation, and voice navigation for learners with reading difficulties are classification problems with labelled datasets and immediate scoring. The Strategy calls for exactly these and notes that assistive algorithm development *must be incentivised* — which is a bounty in all but name.

*Administrative load.* Attendance capture, marking, and routine query handling are verifiable against ground truth and free teacher time in systems the Strategy identifies as facing chronic teacher shortages. The oracle is teacher hours recovered, which is measurable within a term.

**What should not be attempted recursively** is optimisation of instructional sequence or content against learning outcomes. That requires either randomised trials at the scale of the agricultural network in section 20.1 — which is possible, and is the right long-term design — or it requires accepting a fast proxy that will be gamed. Of these, the distributed-trial route is correct and slow; the proxy route is fast and harmful.

**Validation is non-negotiable here in a way it is not elsewhere.** The Strategy calls on Member States to *build national and institutional pre-emptive validation mechanisms for AI systems to be deployed in education*, and to set data protection and age limits for generative systems used by children. Any benchmark family in this domain must therefore carry a child-safety and data-protection gate ahead of deployment, not after. Education is the one priority sector where a system that scores well and ships fast is more dangerous than one that ships late.

## 21. The Slow and Non-Recursive Domains

### 21.1 Governance

Governance has no oracle. There is no function taking a proposed institutional reform and returning a score. Outcomes are contested, multi-causal, slow and unrepeatable.

**What AI genuinely does here:** synthesise evidence across policy literature and administrative records; simulate first-order fiscal and distributional consequences; detect anomalies in procurement, payroll and expenditure data — a real and immediately valuable application; make legal and regulatory material accessible in local languages; improve service delivery at the administrative layer.

**What it cannot do:** discover better institutions through recursive search. Framing governance as a recursive research problem is a category error that will consume resources and produce discredit.

### 21.2 Poverty

More sharply the same. Poverty is not a technical problem with an undiscovered solution; it is a distributional and political outcome. AI contributes through *every other domain* — cheaper energy, better logistics, higher yields, functioning diagnostics, affordable housing — and through better targeting and delivery of transfers.

**Poverty reduction is the aggregate outcome this programme is for. It is the objective function, not a research domain.** Treating it as a domain confuses ends with means.

## 22. The Five Layers of the Build

### Layer 1 — Benchmarks (First; Cheapest; Highest Leverage)

**What.** A published suite of well-specified, machine-scorable tasks across priority domains, with public leaderboards, held-out test sets and clear protocols. Target: 200 tasks within three years.

**Why first.** Nothing compounds without a scoreboard. Benchmarks define what "better" means, make progress legible to funders, allow comparison, and recruit global research effort at zero marginal cost.

**Design requirements.** Tasks must be *machine-scorable* without human judgement, or the oracle property is lost. *Realistic*, drawn from operational data. Test sets *held out and protected* against contamination — Epoch's warning that public test sets are easier to optimise against applies directly here and argues for a private held-out split from day one (WhatLLM 2026). Metrics must reflect deployment constraints including inference cost, latency on modest hardware, and performance under data scarcity — not merely accuracy. And *maintained*, with versioning and refresh.

**Governance.** A continental benchmark authority, thinly staffed but institutionally durable, with domain working groups and an open contribution process. Its most important function is protecting test set integrity.

### Layer 2 — Data Commons

**What.** Curated, licensed, documented datasets: soil and agronomic records, weather and climate reanalysis, disease surveillance, clinical imaging with labels, electricity load and generation telemetry, freight movement and border transit, satellite and drone archives, language corpora in text and speech, materials property data.

**Why second.** Benchmarks require data. Fine-tuning requires data. Evaluation requires data.

**Design requirements.** Common metadata standards; clear licensing per section 15; privacy-preserving access for sensitive categories — differential privacy, federated access, synthetic derivatives; sustainable curation funding, the failure mode that kills most data initiatives; and deliberate gap-filling collection rather than passive aggregation.

**Language data deserves emphasis.** African language corpora are the most acute gap and highest return, because language capability gates every voice-first deployment across health, agriculture and government. Collection is labour-intensive but low-skill and highly distributable — well-suited to broad participation and to the same cooperative structures described in section 20.

### Layer 3 — Physical Experimentation Capacity

**What.** Remotely operable automated laboratories converting digital hypotheses into physical results without a researcher present. Two to four continental facilities, cloud-queued, running continuously.

*Materials and construction testing* — automated characterisation of local building materials, soil-stabiliser combinations, agri-waste composites, bamboo. Cheapest to build, and directly unlocks the housing domain, which currently lacks the property data its simulations require.

*Agricultural phenotyping and soils* — high-throughput soil analysis and controlled-environment plant phenotyping, the fast in-lab complement to the slow field network.

*Biological and diagnostic assay* — automated assay for diagnostic development and, longer term, therapeutic screening against locally prevalent disease.

**Why third.** Capital-intensive and productive only once there is a hypothesis queue — which requires layers one and two. Built too early they sit idle; too late they bottleneck everything.

**Why they matter.** These convert a fast *digital* loop into a real-world one. Without them, agent-generated hypotheses accumulate untested.

**Financing note.** This is the correct destination for capital that would otherwise fund a sovereign frontier training run. Comparable in cost, does not depreciate on a three-year cycle, and produces capability nobody else will.

### Layer 4 — Distributed Field Networks

**What.** Instrumented real-world networks generating continuous experimental data: cooperative trial plots, minigrid telemetry, clinic diagnostic records, freight GPS traces, network measurement, building performance monitoring.

**Why.** The fidelity layer. Simulations and laboratory results predict; field networks verify. Without them the programme optimises against models rather than reality — the classic failure of computational research.

**Non-negotiable conditions.** Informed consent in the participant's language; compensation for risk borne; data rights retained by the community; priority access to results; genuine benefit-sharing.

### Layer 5 — Agent Scaffolding (Last)

**What.** Systems connecting models to oracles: tool-use harnesses, experiment queue management, hypothesis tracking, result interpretation, literature integration, orchestration.

**Why last.** Commoditising faster than any other component; improving monthly; largely open source; built by well-resourced actors globally for their own reasons. Investment depreciates immediately.

**Worth building domestically:** integration with the specific oracles built in layers one to four, which nobody else will do; safety and validity checks appropriate to physical experimentation; multilingual interfaces so domain experts who are not English-speaking ML engineers can direct research; and cost management, since agentic workflows consume inference at rates that matter under African budget constraints.

**Not worth building:** general-purpose agent frameworks, base models, or orchestration infrastructure that already exists openly.

## 24. Delivering Capability Objectives Under Fiscal Concentration

### 24.1 What the Strategy Already Mandates

The Continental AI Strategy directs the AU Commission to *conduct an inventory of existing Centres of Excellence, Research Centres and Think Tanks dealing with AI across the continent and facilitate cooperation among AU Member States in the field of AI research and innovation*, and directs Member States to develop national AI strategies and national AI talent pools. Under each priority sector — agriculture, health, education, climate adaptation — the Strategy calls for designating centres of excellence to build a knowledge base of use cases and facilitate the sharing of experience.

**This section is not an argument against those instruments.** The designated centres of excellence are the correct hosts for the domain working groups described in section 25, and the national strategies are the correct vehicle for the open-weight and data-licensing commitments in Part III. The argument here concerns a narrower and purely practical question: *what happens if capability is built fifty-five times in parallel rather than once and shared.*

### 24.2 The Delivery Constraint

The evidence that parallel national build-out will not deliver is already on the record. Analysis of the first eighteen months of Strategy implementation found **83% of funding concentrated in four countries** against continental needs estimated at roughly $500 billion (Bello 2025). The Strategy's own Call for Action anticipates this, instructing the Commission to develop an implementation plan that *considers the variations and disparities between AU Member States in key capabilities* and *different levels of development and digital readiness.*

Under that observed distribution, a per-country institutional model produces a predictable pattern in the fifty-one states outside the funded four:

- **Sub-scale.** No individual country in that group has the researcher density for critical mass across even one priority domain, let alone the four the Strategy names.
- **Duplicative.** A dozen countries funding overlapping work, none reaching depth, at a moment when the Strategy explicitly seeks *economy of scale for products designed in Africa.*
- **Input-funded.** Budget allocated for existing rather than for achieving, severing resource from result.
- **Isolated.** The Strategy itself identifies the problem: *there are no mechanisms for collaboration and knowledge exchange between researchers, academia and innovators.* An institute without such mechanisms inherits the isolation rather than curing it.

The characteristic outcome is well documented across sectors and geographies: a building, a director, a launch event, a small permanent staff, and slow decline into administering a budget.

### 24.3 The Reconciliation

The proposal in section 25 is therefore best read as **the delivery mechanism for AU capability objectives under the fiscal reality the Strategy anticipates**, not as an alternative to them:

| Strategy instrument | How the problem-indexed network delivers it |
|---|---|
| Designated centres of excellence per sector | Become the domain working groups — defining benchmark tasks and validating field results, which is the knowledge-base function the Strategy asks of them, made measurable |
| National AI strategies | Remain the vehicle for open-weight policy, data licensing and regulatory sandboxes; no continental substitute is proposed |
| National talent pools | Compute allocation floors for early-career and small-institution researchers distribute capability toward the fifty-one, not the four |
| Inventory of research centres | Becomes the node registry — an operating asset rather than a one-off survey |
| Regional data pools (Action Area 6) | Become the data commons, with reciprocity licensing added |
| Challenge-driven research (Action Area 9) | Becomes the benchmark suite and bounty instrument |

**What is genuinely being argued against is only this:** fifty-five separately financed accelerator clusters, and fifty-five separately staffed research institutes each attempting the full domain range. Neither is mandated by the Strategy. Both are the default in the absence of a pooling mechanism, and section 16 sets out why the economics do not support them.

Annex F provides a full crosswalk from this paper's recommendations to the Strategy's fifteen action areas.

## 25. The Problem-Indexed Network

An institutional form organised around **problems and oracles rather than buildings and geography.**

### 25.1 Structure

**A thin continental coordinating body.** Tens of staff, not hundreds. Narrow functions: maintain the benchmark suite and protect test set integrity; curate the data commons and administer licensing; allocate pooled compute through transparent peer review; administer bounty and prize instruments; represent African interests in standards processes. It performs no research. Its legitimacy comes from being useful infrastructure, not from being an authority.

**Distributed research nodes.** Existing universities, institutes, firms and cooperatives competing for compute allocation and prize funding. No new buildings. A node is defined by what it works on, not where it is, and may be three researchers in a department or a hundred-person institute.

**Domain working groups.** For each priority domain, researchers combined with practitioners — agronomists, clinicians, grid operators, logistics managers, builders. Their job is defining benchmark tasks and validating that solutions work in practice. This is the mechanism keeping the programme connected to reality.

**Shared facilities.** Pooled compute (section 16) and self-driving laboratories (layer three), operated professionally, accessed remotely, allocated on merit.

### 25.2 Operating Principles

**Fund outputs, not institutions.** Payment against verified benchmark movement, not proposals and headcount. This makes the network itself recursive: resources flow toward what demonstrably works.

**Publish everything by default.** Benchmarks, data, models, methods, negative results. Leverage comes from recruiting external effort, which requires openness. Commercial exploitation happens at the deployment layer, where local advantage is durable anyway.

**Be radically open to external participation.** A researcher anywhere should be able to work on the benchmarks and win the prizes. Restricting participation to African institutions would reduce effort applied to African problems in order to protect a small quantity of prize money.

**Guarantee a floor for early-career and small-institution researchers.** Merit allocation without a floor concentrates resources in a handful of established groups.

**Sunset relentlessly.** Every workstream carries an explicit termination review. Programmes that cannot demonstrate benchmark movement or field validation end.

## 26. The Political Economy of Capture — and Why This Design Might Survive It

Section 24 argues that parallel national build-out fails through sub-scale, duplication, input funding and isolation — and capture belongs on that list. A design that names these failure modes but does not explain its own immunity is not a design; it is a wish. This section addresses the objection directly, and does not fully defeat it.

### 26.1 The Objection Stated Properly

Continental institutions in Africa are typically staffed by national quota rather than merit, funded by assessed contributions that arrive late or not at all, governed by consensus among members with divergent interests, and structurally unable to terminate anything because termination creates an identifiable loser with a vote. Elite capture is not a risk to be mitigated; it is the **base rate**. The mitigations conventionally offered — transparency, published decisions, participation floors — are precisely the mitigations that have failed elsewhere, and asserting them again is not a response.

### 26.2 Four Structural Features That Change the Incentive Landscape

The argument for this design is not that it is immune. It is that it has four properties most continental programmes lack, each of which removes a specific mechanism through which capture normally operates.

**1. The scoreboard is external and machine-verified.** Capture normally works by controlling the definition of success. Where success is a peer-reviewed proposal, a completed pilot, or a launch event, the definition is negotiable and therefore capturable. Where success is a score on a held-out test set, it is not. A minister cannot negotiate a benchmark score. This is the single most important structural difference, and it is available *only* in the domains where the oracle property holds — which is the deeper reason this paper triages by loop latency rather than by importance. **The method is capture-resistant precisely and only where the oracle is real.**

**2. The prize instrument is open to non-members.** A closed programme distributing funds among member institutions is a distributional bargain, and distributional bargains are captured. A programme in which a laboratory in São Paulo or Bangalore can win the prize creates a permanent external check: any allocation to an African institution that could not have survived open competition is visible as such. This is uncomfortable and it is the point. Section 27.4 addresses the cost of this openness honestly.

**3. The most valuable assets are non-rival and published.** A benchmark suite and a data commons cannot be captured in the way a building or a budget line can, because publishing them destroys their exclusivity value. Once released, a benchmark belongs to everyone; there is no rent to extract from controlling it. The coordinating body's core function — protecting test set integrity — is a *custodial* function with no distributable surplus attached, which is why it can be staffed thinly and why the position is unattractive to patronage networks.

**4. The capital-intensive components are financed externally with commercial governance.** Compute facilities financed by Gulf sovereign capital under commercial terms have an investor with a return requirement and no interest in national quota staffing. This imports a governance discipline that assessed contributions cannot. It also imports a dependency, which section 8 treats honestly; the trade is deliberate.

### 26.3 What This Does Not Solve

Three vulnerabilities remain, and no design in this paper closes them.

**Benchmark selection is capturable even when scoring is not.** Deciding *which* two hundred tasks constitute the suite is a discretionary act with distributional consequences — a country whose priority crop or disease burden is excluded is disadvantaged. The domain working groups are the mitigation, and they are a weak one. The honest response is that this is a smaller and more visible discretion than allocating a research budget, not that it is absent.

**Field validation reintroduces human judgement.** The prize instrument is capture-resistant at the benchmark stage and much less so at the field validation stage, where verification requires site visits, clinical judgement, and contextual assessment. Since field validation is where the larger prizes sit, this is where corruption pressure will concentrate. Independent verification with rotating external assessors is the mitigation; it is standard and imperfect.

**Sunset provisions are only as strong as the body applying them.** A coordinating body dependent on member state contributions will find terminating a member state's workstream difficult. The partial answer is to make sunset *automatic on failure to meet a published threshold* rather than discretionary — removing the need for anyone to make a decision that creates a loser. This works only if the threshold is set before the workstream begins and is not renegotiable, which requires it to be written into the founding instrument rather than into policy.

### 26.4 The Realistic Claim

The claim is not that this design escapes African institutional political economy. It is that **the oracle property does real work against capture, in the specific domains where it holds, and does none at all where it does not.** This is a further and independent reason to concentrate the programme on fast-loop domains and to refuse to extend the method to governance and poverty — not merely because the science does not work there, but because the *institutional protection* does not work there either.

A programme that expands into domains without oracles will be captured, and the capture will discredit the parts that were working.

## 27. Financing Instruments and Costed Requirements

### 27.1 Why Grants Are the Wrong Default

Traditional grants pay for *proposals* — for the persuasiveness of a plan, assessed before work is done, by reviewers who cannot know whether it will work. Consequences: incentive to over-promise, bias toward established groups skilled at proposal writing, no penalty for failure to deliver, heavy administrative overhead on both sides.

Where a verifiable oracle exists, this is unnecessary. Payment can be made against measured movement on a published benchmark, or against verified field performance.

### 27.2 The Instrument Portfolio

**Benchmark bounties.** A published prize for a specified score on a specified benchmark, open to anyone, verified automatically against the held-out test set, near-zero administrative cost. Default instrument for fast-loop domains.

**Field validation prizes.** Larger prizes for demonstrated real-world performance — a diagnostic achieving specified sensitivity and specificity in prospective clinical use; a dispatch system achieving specified cost reduction across a specified number of deployed sites; a housing design achieving specified cost and thermal performance in built and occupied units. Slower to verify, but this is what converts benchmark performance into value.

**Advance market commitments.** Binding commitment to purchase specified quantity at specified price if a product meeting specified criteria is developed. Strong record in vaccine development; well-suited to diagnostics, agricultural inputs, construction components and energy hardware. Shifts risk from developer to purchaser without paying for failure. Natural instrument for Afreximbank and AfDB.

**Infrastructure grants.** For the benchmark suite, data commons, laboratories and field networks — genuine public goods with no revenue model, requiring patient, unconditional, long-horizon funding. Appropriate for multilateral and philanthropic capital, because no commercial actor will fund it.

**Blended structures for deployment.** Once something works, scaling is a commercial proposition requiring commercial capital with concessional first-loss. Appropriate role for development finance institutions and for Gulf capital.

### 27.3 Costed Requirements

Annex B provides the bottom-up build. Summary of annual and capital requirements at continental scale, with comparator programmes:

| Component | Capital (USD) | Annual operating (USD) | Basis |
|---|---|---|---|
| Benchmark authority + 200-task suite | ~$4m | **$14–22m** | 18 staff; 200 tasks at $40–90k each amortised; compute for baseline evaluation |
| Data commons (incl. language corpora) | ~$6m | **$25–40m** | Curation labour dominant; 10,000 speech hours across 40 languages at $25–40/hr |
| Materials testing laboratory | **$18–30m** | $4–6m | One facility; comparator: university-scale automated materials lab |

**Phase 1 total (Years 1–2), excluding compute:** approximately **$18m capital and $50–70m annually.**

**Full programme at maturity (Years 5–10), including three compute facilities:** approximately **$700m–1.4bn capital and $200–350m annually.**

### 27.4 Reading These Numbers

Three observations matter more than the figures.

**Phase 1 is affordable now.** $50–70 million annually is small against the $500 billion continental AI need estimated for Continental AI Strategy delivery (Bello 2025), against the $23 billion Gulf sovereign funds deployed into AI and digitalisation in 2025, and against Africa's ~$80 billion annual infrastructure financing gap. It is within reach of a single substantial philanthropic commitment, a modest multilateral allocation, or a pooled contribution from four or five member states. **The highest-leverage components of this programme do not require the capital-intensive components to be financed first, and should not wait for them.**

**The laboratory layer is the correct substitute for a sovereign model.** A frontier training run and a biological assay facility cost the same order of magnitude. One depreciates in twenty-four months; the other produces capability for twenty years and generates data nobody else will generate.

**The open prize model has a distributional cost that must be stated.** Prize instruments transfer risk to the solver, structurally advantaging well-capitalised solvers who can absorb unpaid failure. Open global participation therefore plausibly means a significant share of prizes flows to institutions outside Africa — African public money funding non-African capability accumulation.

This paper accepts that trade with two specific corrections rather than pretending it away. First, **field validation prizes should carry a local-partnership requirement**: real-world deployment in an African context requires an African implementing partner regardless, so requiring one is not protectionism but a description of how deployment works. Second, **the benchmark and data layers should carry no participation restriction at all**, because the objective there is maximum external effort, and the asset created — the benchmark, the corpus, the resulting open model — remains African-owned regardless of who wins.

The net position: Africa pays some prize money outward and retains the oracle, the data, the deployment channel and the domain relationships. That is a favourable trade, but it is a trade, and boards approving it should approve it knowingly.

## 28. Standards Influence: A Designed Capability, Not an Aspiration

Section 6 diagnoses Africa as a rule-taker in European regulatory processes. Section 11 asserts that bloc negotiation is the remedy. Assertion is not design. This section specifies what the capability actually consists of, because it is the one recommendation in this paper that requires no new money and delivers the fastest return.

Standards influence has four components, in order of tractability.

**1. Technical evidence generation.** Regulatory processes respond to evidence, and the party that supplies evidence shapes outcomes. The deforestation regulation's core difficulty is that it assumes geospatial data and tenure formality that smallholder systems do not produce — a claim that is currently made rhetorically and could be made quantitatively. A continental capability that can demonstrate, with plot-level data across multiple countries, the exact distribution of compliance cost by farm size converts a grievance into a technical submission. The environmental monitoring capability described in section 20.2 produces exactly this evidence as a by-product. **This is the highest-return use of the data commons in the first three years.**

**2. Compliance systems built on African terms.** Where a standard cannot be changed, the cost of meeting it can be. Traceability, provenance and verification systems built once as open continental infrastructure — rather than fifty-four times as national systems, or purchased as a service from foreign vendors — reduce compliance cost by an order of magnitude and keep the resulting data in African jurisdiction. This is a direct commercial application of the open-weights doctrine.

**3. Coordinated technical presence in standards bodies.** Influence in ISO, ITU, Codex and equivalent AI governance fora is a function of sustained technical participation by people who attend every meeting and read every draft, not of ministerial statements. This is cheap — a coordinated continental delegation function of perhaps fifteen to twenty technical specialists — and almost entirely unfunded. The AU STC on Communication and ICT is the natural home.

**4. Reciprocal standard-setting.** Africa sets standards too, and does not use them. A continental market of $3.4 trillion can impose conditions on entry — including the data reciprocity obligation of section 15.2, and including performance and evaluation requirements for AI systems deployed in African health and financial services. The EU's leverage derives entirely from market access; the same instrument is available and unused.

**Sequencing.** Components 1 and 3 are affordable immediately and should begin in Year 1. Component 2 follows the data commons. Component 4 requires the market integration that AfCFTA is delivering unevenly, and is a Year 5+ proposition.

# Part VI — Distribution and Labour

## 29. Who Gains: The Displacement Question

The largest risk this programme carries is not failure but **narrow success** — raising productivity while eroding the labour-intensive pathways through which young workforces have historically been absorbed. The earlier version of this paper named this the top risk and then deferred it. That was inconsistent, and this section corrects it.

### 29.1 Why This Is Sharper for Africa Than Elsewhere

Africa has a median age of 19 and the world's fastest-growing labour force. The historical absorption pathways — labour-intensive manufacturing, then services, then business process outsourcing — are precisely the sectors where AI substitution pressure is most direct. A high-income country facing AI-driven displacement has a shrinking workforce and a fiscal base for transfers. Africa has neither.

This is also, and not coincidentally, **the strategic problem India shares** — a young population, a services-led development model, and an AI transition that threatens the services rungs of the ladder. It is the single strongest case for joint policy research rather than imported analysis, because neither party has the answer and the existing literature is written by and for economies in the opposite demographic position.

### 29.2 Why the Domain Selection Partly Answers It

The prioritisation in Part IV was made on loop latency, but it has a distributional property worth making explicit: **the fast-loop domains are largely complement-heavy rather than substitute-heavy.**

- Better logistics does not replace traders and hauliers; it increases the volume of goods worth moving. Post-harvest loss reduction converts destroyed output into sold output, which is additional economic activity requiring additional labour.
- Cheaper energy expands the set of viable productive activities. Minigrid economics depend on *productive use* load — the explicit design objective is more economic activity, not less labour per unit.
- Affordable housing expands construction volume, which is labour-intensive and largely non-tradeable.
- Diagnostic AI in specialist-scarce settings substitutes for a specialist who is *absent*, not one who is employed. This is capability creation, not displacement.
- Connectivity extension is straightforwardly complementary.

The substitution-heavy applications — document processing, customer service, routine analysis, translation, basic coding — are real and will arrive regardless of what this programme does. They are not what this programme funds.

**This is not an accident to be claimed as foresight; it is a fortunate property of triaging by loop latency.** But it is a real property, and it should inform how the programme is defended politically.

### 29.3 Design Choices That Distribute Gains

**Cooperative ownership of the field networks.** Section 20.1 specifies that data rights remain with the cooperative. This is not only an ethics provision — it is a distributional one. The cooperative that hosts the trial network holds an asset that appreciates, and the value of the resulting varieties, practices and models flows to the participants rather than around them.

**Advance market commitments favour local manufacture.** An AMC for diagnostic devices, construction components or agricultural inputs can specify local manufacture as a condition of purchase. This is the pharmaceutical template applied deliberately.

**Skills sited in Africa, not exported.** The IIT Madras Zanzibar campus is the model worth replicating — training capacity located in-country rather than students exported. The reverse flow is what the US relationship delivers, and it is why section 7 scores it poorly.

**Deployment-layer competition is where the jobs are.** The open-weights doctrine's economic point is that value accrues at the deployment layer, which is labour-intensive: last-mile delivery, trust relationships, contextual adaptation, service provision. A continent that consumes closed AI as a service captures none of this employment. A continent that deploys open models domestically captures most of it.

### 29.4 What Should Be Monitored

Assumed benefit is how displacement programmes fail. Section 32 includes labour-outcome indicators for this reason. The specific things to watch: employment intensity per unit of output in the sectors the programme touches; whether cooperative participants' incomes rise or only their yields; and whether deployment-layer firms are forming domestically or whether the layer is being served from abroad.

If the second and third indicators go the wrong way, the programme is generating productivity without distribution, and the design requires revision rather than expansion.

## 30. Governance, Ethics, and Failure Modes

### 30.1 Adopting the Continental Ethical Framework Rather Than Inventing One

This paper does not propose an ethical framework. One exists, it was adopted by fifty-five Member States, and it is more developed on inclusion than anything this paper would produce independently. The Continental AI Strategy's guiding principles — Local First, people-centred, human rights and human dignity, peace and prosperity, inclusion and diversity, ethics and transparency, cooperation and integration, skills development — govern this programme, and the following provisions apply to it directly rather than by analogy.

**Human rights foundation.** The African Charter on Human and Peoples' Rights and its subsidiary instruments, together with ACHPR Resolution 473 on AI, robotics and other emerging technologies, are the binding reference. Resolution 473's requirement that meaningful human control over AI be maintained and codified as a human rights principle applies with particular force to a programme proposing autonomous experimentation: **no experimental loop in this programme may terminate in an action affecting a person without human authorisation**, regardless of how good the oracle is.

**Inclusion and diversity as a design constraint, not a review stage.** The Strategy is explicit that AI in Africa must be *non-discriminatory, leaving no one and no place behind*, and names the groups at risk: women and girls, persons with disabilities, migrants, children, rural and remote populations, and people on low incomes. Three operational consequences for this programme:

- **Benchmark composition must be audited for representation.** A diagnostic benchmark drawn only from urban tertiary hospitals encodes an urban tertiary population. Every benchmark family in Annex A carries a representation statement declaring which populations its data covers and which it does not, published alongside the leaderboard.
- **Gender parity in the compute allocation floor.** The floor for early-career and small-institution researchers described in section 25.2 is meaningless if it reproduces existing gender composition. It should be specified with a distributional target, reported publicly, and treated as a condition of the coordinating body's mandate rather than an aspiration.
- **Disability and accessibility as first-class benchmark targets**, per the Strategy's call for assistive algorithm development to be incentivised. Section 20.3 places these in the education domain; they belong equally in health and public service delivery.

**Indigenous knowledge and cultural heritage.** The Strategy identifies the *subversion of indigenous knowledge and African cultural heritage* as a distinct category of risk — appropriation, misrepresentation, and the marginalisation of communities whose knowledge systems are inadequately represented in models. This paper's agricultural programme is directly exposed: smallholder agronomic practice *is* indigenous knowledge, and a distributed trial network that extracts it into a model without attribution or benefit is precisely the failure the Strategy names. The community-consent and benefit-sharing provisions in sections 15.1 and 20.1 are therefore not merely ethical hygiene; they are the specific mitigation for a specific, named continental risk, and should be drafted with communities rather than for them.

**Children.** The Strategy calls for promotion of the protection of children in the face of AI, and the AU Child Online Safety and Empowerment Policy applies. Section 20.3 sets the gate for education deployments; the same gate applies to any system in this programme with a plausible child user.

**Ubuntu and the collective.** The Strategy names Ubuntu — respect for collective community over individuality — among the values AI in Africa must respect. This has a concrete implication that Western data-governance frameworks handle badly: **consent may be individually given and collectively consequential.** A farmer consenting to share plot data discloses information about a community's practices and land. The consent architecture in section 15.1 must therefore include a community tier, and this paper acknowledged in that section that the community tier is the less-solved problem. It remains so, and it is the single most important open design question in the programme's data regime.

### 30.2 Governance Instruments to Adopt

Rather than construct parallel machinery, the programme should use the instruments the Strategy already specifies:

| Strategy instrument | Application here |
|---|---|
| UNESCO Ethical Impact Assessment | Mandatory before any field validation prize is paid |
| UNESCO Readiness Assessment Methodology | Baseline for participating Member States |
| Regional independent AI Ethics Board | Reviews any proposal for large-scale autonomous experimentation |
| Algorithmic transparency registers | The benchmark leaderboard is one; deployed systems arising from it must be registered |
| Regulatory sandboxes | The mechanism for connectivity spectrum trials (19.2) and education deployments (20.3) |
| Public procurement standards for AI | Governs how Member States buy anything the programme produces |
| African Observatory on Responsible AI | Independent external review function, per the Strategy's instruction that it be leveraged for implementation |

The last matters most for credibility. A programme that scores its own ethics is not scored. The Observatory is an existing, independent, continental body with exactly the remit required, and using it costs the programme nothing but the discomfort of external assessment.

### 30.3 Failure Modes

**Benchmark gaming.** Systems scoring well without solving the problem. Mitigations: protected held-out test sets — with the Epoch warning about public test set optimisation taken seriously and a private split maintained from day one; periodic refresh; mandatory field validation before any large prize; adversarial red-teaming of benchmark integrity.

**Extractive data collection.** Data gathered from farmers, patients and communities generating value elsewhere. Mitigations: the consent, compensation and benefit-sharing conditions in Part IV, as binding programme conditions rather than aspirations.

**Elite capture.** Addressed at length in section 26, including what the design does not solve.

**Deployment without validation.** Benchmark performance mistaken for real-world safety, acute in health. Mitigations: mandatory prospective validation; independent evaluation capacity; regulatory pathways established before deployment.

**The pilot graveyard.** The characteristic failure of development technology. Mitigations: advance market commitments creating demand before supply; deployment-stage financing designed in from the start; evaluating on deployed impact rather than pilots completed.

**Displacement without absorption.** Section 29.

**Institutional decay.** Mitigations: automatic rather than discretionary sunset (section 26.3); a permanently thin coordinating body; output-based funding; periodic external review with consequences.

**Safety obligations.** A continent deploying open weights at scale in health, agriculture, energy and government acquires safety obligations that cannot be outsourced to a vendor — particularly given AISI's finding that safety measures on open models are largely ineffective against determined modification (The Decoder 2026). This requires domestic capacity in contextual model evaluation, deployed-system monitoring, incident response and pre-deployment red-teaming.

This capacity is not a burden bolted on. It is the *same capability* as evaluation sovereignty and as benchmark credibility. Build it once, use it three ways.

# Part VII — Execution

## 31. A Ten-Year Sequenced Roadmap

### Years 1–2: Foundation
*Cost: ~$18m capital, $50–70m annually*

*Partnership.* Negotiate a structured India–Africa technology framework covering DPI reference implementation with explicit open-source and safeguard conditions, joint low-resource-language research, health and pharmaceutical technology transfer, and skills capacity sited in Africa on the IIT Zanzibar model. Test the MOSIP/UPI openness distinction (section 12.4) explicitly in negotiation. Conclude Gulf financing terms for regional compute with ownership and governance fixed at the outset. Publish the portfolio role assignment.

*Leverage model.* Adopt open-weight-first policy for sovereign digital functions. Insert the reciprocity obligation into the existing AfCFTA Digital Trade Protocol implementation rather than drafting new instruments. Begin harmonisation of national data regimes.

*Research architecture.* Constitute the benchmark authority under the Continental AI Strategy implementation phase, hosted by AUDA-NEPAD. Publish the first fifty tasks in logistics, connectivity, energy and diagnostics. Begin data commons curation prioritising language corpora and clinical imaging. Commission the freight and trade digital twin as decision support for PIDA corridor prioritisation. Build the materials testing facility.

*Standards.* Stand up the technical delegation function (section 28, components 1 and 3). Begin plot-level compliance-cost evidence generation.

*Institutional.* Coordinating body established with hard staffing cap and automatic sunset thresholds written into the founding instrument. Domain working groups constituted with practitioner majorities. First benchmark bounties at modest scale to prove the instrument.

### Years 3–5: Compounding
*Cost: ~$250–450m capital, $120–200m annually*

*Partnership.* First DPI implementations operational in early-adopter states with exclusion safeguards audited. Joint India–Africa language model releases. Pharmaceutical and diagnostic manufacturing technology transfer under way.

*Leverage model.* First regional compute facility operational, sited on renewable generation, peer-reviewed allocation running. Data licensing generating both revenue and reciprocal open model releases.

*Research architecture.* Benchmark suite at 150 tasks with active global participation and visible leaderboard movement. Agricultural distributed trial network operational across multiple agro-ecological zones. Agricultural phenotyping and biological assay laboratories operational. First field validation prizes paid. Freight twin calibrated and producing corridor recommendations that are acted upon.

*Institutional.* First automatic sunset triggered, with at least one workstream terminated — the test of whether the discipline is real. Bounty instrument scaled on demonstrated results.

### Years 6–10: Harvest
*Cost: ~$400m–900m residual capital, $200–350m annually*

*Partnership.* Portfolio matured, African negotiating capacity strengthened by a functioning single digital market and demonstrated technical capability.

*Leverage model.* Federated regional compute operational. African-fine-tuned open models deployed at scale across health, agriculture, energy and government. Data regime enforced through collective market access leverage.

*Research architecture.* 200-plus benchmark suite with sustained global participation. Documented inventions — construction materials and typologies, diagnostic tools, crop varieties, dispatch systems, logistics designs — traceable to the programme and deployed at scale. Laboratories running continuous agent-directed experimentation, contingent on the agent capability question in section 12.1 resolving favourably.

*Institutional.* Network self-sustaining through data licensing revenue, commercial compute cross-subsidy, and continued public and philanthropic support justified by verified results.

## 32. Metrics That Would Show This Is Working

Vanity metrics — papers published, pilots launched, memoranda signed, people trained — should be avoided.

**Leading indicators (Years 1–3)**
- Published, actively-used benchmark tasks with external submissions
- **Non-African research groups submitting to African benchmarks** — measures whether the leverage mechanism works
- Curated data volume, particularly language hours by language
- Compute allocated to early-career and small-institution researchers as a share of total
- Data licensing agreements concluded with reciprocal open-release obligations
- Technical submissions made to external standards processes

**Intermediate indicators (Years 3–6)**
- Measured benchmark score movement by domain
- **Field validation prizes paid** — the count of things that worked in the world, not the laboratory
- Laboratory utilisation and experiment throughput
- Trial network coverage across agro-ecological zones and participating households
- Share of sovereign digital functions running on open, domestically-operated models
- Share of prize value won by African-led teams (against the section 27.4 trade)

**Outcome indicators (Years 5–10)**
- Continental logistics cost per tonne-kilometre
- **Post-harvest loss rate for priority commodities** — baseline 30–50%
- Cost per kilowatt-hour delivered by minigrids
- Cost per square metre of formal housing meeting defined performance standards
- Diagnostic coverage in specialist-scarce populations
- **Intra-African trade as a share of total African trade** — baseline ~16%, benchmark Asia 59–60%, Europe 68%
- Employment intensity per unit output in programme-touched sectors (section 29.4)
- Cooperative participant income, not merely yield
- **Researcher retention** — share of African-trained AI researchers working in Africa five years after training

The last may be the single most informative number here. If the programme builds genuine capability, people stay. If not, they leave, and everything else is noise.

## 33. Conclusion: Abundance Is an Institutional Question

**On partnership.** India is the most strategically aligned bilateral partner for Africa in the AI era — not because it offers the most, but because it is solving the same problem under the same constraints, and because its capability transfer record in the layer that matters is documented rather than promised: roughly sixteen of twenty-nine MOSIP country engagements in Africa with thirteen national rollouts under way, Togo's identity system co-built with IIIT-Bangalore, Namibia's UPI licence, Kenya's DigiLocker adoption, IIT Madras in Zanzibar. Capital should come from the Gulf, hardware from China, market access from the European Union, frontier research linkage from the United States. But the *architecture* should be built with the partner whose interests are structurally aligned with African capability rather than African dependence. **Capital and hardware are fungible. Architecture is not.**

**On leverage.** Open weights have collapsed the cost of capability toward zero — the gap to the closed frontier now measured in months rather than years — leaving the scarce inputs as data, deployment, domain knowledge and trust, all of which Africa holds and none of which can be acquired from outside. The strategy is not to compete on pre-training, which is lost on arithmetic, but to own the complements, which is winnable and compounds.

**On method.** Recursive improvement requires an oracle. Most African development problems do not have one. The work is to manufacture oracles — benchmarks, simulators, laboratories, instrumented field networks, data commons — sequenced by how fast each loop can be made to close. Logistics, connectivity, energy, housing and diagnostics first. Agriculture through breadth rather than speed. Governance and poverty through synthesis rather than search, because they have no ground truth — and because, as section 26 argues, the institutional protection against capture also fails exactly where the oracle does.

Agents are the last layer to build, not the first. Oracles will not build themselves, and nobody outside Africa will build them.

**And the precondition governing everything:** none of this survives fragmentation. Intra-African trade at 16% against Asia's 59% and Europe's 68%; 83% of continental AI funding concentrated in four countries; fifty-four data regimes, fifty-four procurement processes, fifty-four undersized compute ambitions. The single digital market is not an adjacent agenda that would be nice to complete alongside this one. It is the condition under which any of this has effect.

The instruments exist. The Continental AI Strategy is adopted. The Kigali Declaration is signed by 49 states. The Digital Trade Protocol is in force. PAPSS is operational. What is missing is not mandate but a specific, cheap, sequenced first move that produces verifiable results inside a single funding cycle.

Phase 1 of that move costs roughly $50 to $70 million a year.

The age of abundance, if it arrives, will not arrive because the models became good enough. They are already good enough for a great deal of what is described here. It will arrive because the institutions that convert capability into value were built during the window in which building them was still possible.

That window is open now. It has been open three times before, and each time it closed on terms set elsewhere.

### Where this paper diverges from the Strategy, and how it should be read

Three divergences are substantive and are stated here rather than left for a reviewer to find.

**1. Sector selection.** The Strategy names agriculture, health, education, culture and climate as priority sectors on grounds of social and economic value. This paper leads with logistics, connectivity, energy and housing — which the Strategy treats as *enablers* under Action Area 6 rather than as application domains. The argument for the divergence is tractability, not importance: these domains have fast, machine-verifiable feedback loops and are therefore where a research programme produces results inside a funding cycle. **The recommendation is additive, not substitutive** — the Strategy's priority sectors remain the objective; the enabler domains are proposed as the sequence through which capability to serve them is built.

**2. Institutional form.** Addressed at length in §24. The Strategy's designated centres of excellence and national strategies are retained; what is argued against is fifty-five separately financed compute clusters and fifty-five full-range research institutes, neither of which the Strategy mandates.

**3. Open weights.** The Strategy's capability model — adopted July 2024 — is *acquire datasets and computing platforms*. It refers extensively to open **data** and almost not at all to open **models**. This paper argues the open-weight convergence measured since (Epoch AI 2026; AISI 2026) materially changes the cost of the capability layer and therefore the optimal sequencing of investment. This is not a disagreement with the Strategy; it is new evidence arriving after adoption, and it is the principal reason this paper is offered to the implementation phase rather than as commentary on the strategy phase.

### Gap this paper does not fill

**Action Area 7 — information integrity, media and information literacy** — is unaddressed. It is a domain without a clean oracle (truth is contested, and a benchmark for "accurate information" would be captured immediately), which is consistent with this paper's own triage logic in §21. It is nonetheless a named continental priority and a serious one, and the appropriate response is that it requires a different method rather than that it matters less.

## Annex E — Glossary

**Advance market commitment** — A binding commitment to purchase a specified quantity of a product at a specified price if it is developed to defined specifications, shifting development risk without paying for failure.

**AfCFTA** — African Continental Free Trade Area. Operational since January 2021; $3.4 trillion market across 54 economies.

**Digital Public Infrastructure (DPI)** — Foundational digital systems — identity, payments, data exchange — provided as public rails on which private services compete, rather than as private platforms extracting rent.

**ECI (Epoch Capability Index)** — Epoch AI's composite measure of model capability across many benchmarks, used here to quantify the open–closed gap.

**Fine-tuning** — Adapting a pre-trained model to a specific task or domain using a comparatively small dataset, at a small fraction of pre-training cost.

**Inference** — Running a trained model to produce output. The dominant ongoing cost of AI deployment, as distinct from the one-time cost of training.

**MOSIP** — Modular Open-Source Identity Platform. Open-source foundational identity architecture developed at IIIT-Bangalore, inspired by India's Aadhaar; of twenty-nine country engagements, approximately sixteen are African, with thirteen national rollouts under way.

**Open weights** — Model parameters released under a licence permitting download, inspection, modification and redistribution, enabling operation on infrastructure under the user's control.

**Oracle** — A mechanism that scores a proposed solution without human judgement in the loop, characterised by latency, cost per evaluation, and fidelity to real-world performance. The binding constraint on recursive improvement.

**PAPSS** — Pan-African Payment and Settlement System. Operational since 2022; delivers savings of roughly 27% against traditional correspondent banking, but connects only nineteen of fifty-four African countries as of early 2026.

**Recursive improvement** — A loop in which a system proposes solutions, receives scores, updates, and proposes better solutions, such that progress compounds rather than accumulating linearly. Requires an oracle.

**Self-driving laboratory** — An automated, remotely operable experimental facility that executes physical experiments from digital instructions without a researcher present.

**Structural symmetry** — The degree to which two economies face similar binding constraints, such that solutions developed in one transfer to the other at low adaptation cost.

**UPI** — Unified Payments Interface. India's real-time retail payment architecture; processes 18–20 billion transactions monthly; licensed to Namibia in April 2024 as the first African adoption.
